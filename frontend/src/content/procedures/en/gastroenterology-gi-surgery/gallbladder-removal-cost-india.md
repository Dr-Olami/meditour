## Overview

Being told you need your gallbladder removed can feel unsettling, especially if you have never had surgery before. That is completely understandable. But gallbladder removal — formally called cholecystectomy — is one of the most common and safest abdominal operations performed worldwide, and Bangalore has become a trusted destination for patients who need this procedure done expertly at a fraction of the cost they would pay at home.

Your gallbladder is a small pear-shaped organ that sits under your liver and stores bile, a fluid that helps digest fat. Sometimes, the bile crystallises into hard stones — gallstones — which can block the ducts, cause intense pain (biliary colic), trigger inflammation (cholecystitis), or even lead to pancreatitis. When gallstones become symptomatic, removing the gallbladder is the definitive treatment, because the stones will keep forming as long as the gallbladder is there.

For most patients, the surgery is done laparoscopically — through four tiny keyhole incisions — which means minimal scarring, less pain, and a remarkably quick recovery. Many people walk the same evening and go home within a day or two. Bangalore's accredited hospitals perform thousands of these procedures each year with outcomes that match the best international benchmarks, at prices 80 to 90% lower than in the US or UK.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

You may be reading this because you have had episodes of sharp upper abdominal pain, usually after a meal, and an ultrasound has shown gallstones. That pain — often radiating to your back or right shoulder, accompanied by nausea and bloating — is the classic sign that your gallbladder is no longer functioning well.

Cholecystectomy is recommended if you have **symptomatic gallstones** causing recurrent attacks of biliary colic. Even if the attacks come and go, they tend to worsen over time and can lead to serious complications like acute cholecystitis, where the gallbladder becomes inflamed and infected, or gallstone pancreatitis, which can be life-threatening.

Your doctor may also advise surgery if you have **large gallstones** (greater than 2 cm, which carry a higher risk of complications), **gallbladder polyps** greater than 1 cm (which can occasionally be pre-cancerous), or if you have had a complication like a blocked bile duct that was treated endoscopically. If you have had one episode of gallstone pancreatitis, removing the gallbladder prevents recurrence.

Occasionally, patients with a poorly functioning gallbladder — confirmed on a HIDA scan — but no visible stones also benefit from removal. Your surgeon in Bangalore will review your ultrasound, blood tests, and symptoms to confirm that surgery is the right step for you.

## Pre-operative Preparation

Before you travel to Bangalore, share your ultrasound report, blood test results, and a list of all medications you take with your surgical team. If you have had an MRCP (a specialised MRI of the bile ducts), bring those images too. This allows your surgeon to plan the operation and check for any stones in the bile duct that might need endoscopic removal (ERCP) before surgery.

Your doctor will likely ask you to stop blood-thinning medications — such as warfarin, clopidogrel, or aspirin — several days before surgery to reduce bleeding risk. Be honest about everything you take, including herbal supplements, because some can interfere with anaesthesia. If you smoke, try to stop at least a week before surgery, as smoking increases the risk of wound infections and breathing problems under anaesthesia.

Once you arrive in Bangalore, you will have a pre-operative assessment including blood tests, an ultrasound or repeat imaging, and an anaesthesia consultation. You will be asked to fast for 6 to 8 hours before the procedure. Most patients are scheduled for surgery within 2 to 3 days of arrival.

## Procedure Details

It is natural to feel anxious about what will happen during the surgery. Gallbladder removal is done under general anaesthesia, meaning you will be completely asleep and will not feel anything. The procedure typically takes 45 minutes to 90 minutes, depending on the complexity.

The standard approach is **laparoscopic cholecystectomy**. Your surgeon makes four small incisions — each about 5 to 10 millimetres — in your abdomen. Carbon dioxide gas is gently introduced to create space, and a tiny camera (laparoscope) is inserted to give the surgeon a clear, magnified view of your gallbladder on a high-definition monitor. Specialised instruments are passed through the other ports to carefully detach the gallbladder from the liver, clip and divide the cystic duct and cystic artery, and remove the gallbladder through one of the small incisions.

In about 5 to 10% of cases, the surgeon may need to convert to an **open cholecystectomy** — a larger incision under the right ribs. This is not a complication but a safety decision, made if there is severe inflammation, scarring, or bleeding that makes the laparoscopic approach unsafe. Your surgeon will always prioritise your safety over keeping the procedure minimally invasive.

Some centres in Bangalore also offer **single-incision laparoscopic surgery (SILS)**, where all instruments are passed through one small incision in the navel, leaving virtually no visible scar. This is suitable for selected patients and offers an excellent cosmetic outcome.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the day of your surgery, you will be admitted to the pre-operative area. The nursing team will start your IV line, give you antibiotics, and attach monitoring equipment. The anaesthesiologist will review your details and gently put you to sleep with general anaesthesia. A breathing tube will be inserted to support your breathing. You will not feel or remember any of this.

Once you are asleep, your surgeon cleans your abdomen and makes the small incisions. The laparoscope is inserted, and the surgical team inspects the gallbladder and surrounding structures. The gallbladder is carefully separated from the liver bed, the cystic duct and artery are clipped and divided, and the organ is placed into a retrieval bag and removed. The bile duct is inspected to ensure no stones remain and no bile is leaking.

The gas is released, the incisions are closed with dissolvable stitches, and small dressings are applied. You will be taken to the recovery room, where the anaesthesia team wakes you up gently. Most patients are fully awake within an hour and transferred to their room later that evening.

## Post-operative Care & Recovery

When you wake up, you will be in the recovery room, connected to monitors that track your heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen levels. You may feel some soreness in your abdomen and at the incision sites, and some patients notice mild shoulder pain from the gas used during surgery — this is temporary and usually settles within 24 to 48 hours.

**Will it hurt?** There will be some discomfort, but because the procedure is laparoscopic, the pain is far less than with open surgery. You will receive both IV and oral pain medications, and most patients find the discomfort very manageable. By the second day, many people need only simple oral painkillers.

You will be encouraged to get up and walk the evening of your surgery or the next morning — early mobilisation helps prevent blood clots and speeds up recovery. You will start with sips of water and progress to a light diet. Most laparoscopic patients go home within 24 to 48 hours.

Before discharge, your nurse will teach you how to care for your incisions, what warning signs to watch for, and what diet to follow. A low-fat diet is recommended for the first few weeks, as your body adjusts to digesting food without the gallbladder.

## Long-term Outcomes & Success Rates

You are probably wondering whether removing your gallbladder will affect your digestion long-term. The reassuring answer is that the vast majority of people live completely normal lives without it.

Cholecystectomy has excellent outcomes. The procedure eliminates gallstone-related pain in over 95% of patients, and the risk of recurrence is effectively zero because the gallbladder — the organ that forms the stones — has been removed. Complication rates are low, with bile duct injury occurring in less than 0.5% of laparoscopic procedures at experienced centres. Bangalore's leading hospitals report outcomes consistent with the best international benchmarks.

After surgery, your liver continues to produce bile, which flows directly into your intestine. Most people return to a normal diet within a few weeks. About 5 to 10% of patients experience **post-cholecystectomy syndrome** — lingering bloating, indigestion, or loose stools after fatty meals — which usually settles within a few months as the digestive system adapts. There is no impact on your lifespan, and you can resume all normal activities, including exercise, within a couple of weeks.

## Follow-up Care After Returning Home

Before you leave Bangalore, you will receive a comprehensive discharge summary detailing the procedure, pathology report (if the gallbladder was sent for testing), medications prescribed, and follow-up recommendations. Share this with your doctor back home.

A follow-up review is typically scheduled at 2 to 4 weeks after surgery. If your gallbladder was sent for histopathology — which is standard practice — the report will confirm whether there were any unexpected findings, such as inflammation or, very rarely, early cancer. Most reports show chronic cholecystitis, which is benign.

Watch for warning signs after you return home: fever, worsening abdominal pain, jaundice (yellowing of the eyes or skin), persistent nausea or vomiting, or redness and discharge at the incision sites. ## Alternatives to Consider

You might be wondering if there is a way to treat gallstones without surgery. The honest answer is that non-surgical options exist but are rarely effective long-term.

**Medication to dissolve gallstones** (ursodeoxycholic acid) can work for small, cholesterol-based stones, but it takes months to years, does not work for all stone types, and stones usually return once the medication is stopped — because the gallbladder is still there to form them.

**Shock wave lithotripsy** can break stones into fragments, but again, recurrence is common. These approaches are generally reserved for patients who cannot have surgery due to severe medical problems.

If your symptoms are mild and infrequent, your doctor may suggest a period of observation and dietary modification (avoiding fatty, fried foods). However, symptomatic gallstones tend to progress, and delaying surgery increases the risk of complications like acute cholecystitis, pancreatitis, or jaundice from a blocked bile duct. For most patients, laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the safest and most definitive solution.
