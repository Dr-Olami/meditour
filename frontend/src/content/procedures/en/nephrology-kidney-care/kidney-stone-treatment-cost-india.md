## Overview

Kidney stones are hard deposits of minerals and salts that form inside your kidneys. They can be as small as a grain of sand or as large as a golf ball, and when they move through your urinary tract, they can cause some of the most severe pain a person can experience. If you have a kidney stone that is too large to pass on its own, is causing persistent pain, blocking urine flow, or damaging your kidney, medical treatment becomes necessary.

Kidney stone treatment in India offers international patients an affordable, high-quality solution. Procedures range from $1,500 to $5,000 in Bangalore, compared to $10,000-$30,000 in the United States or $6,000-$15,000 in the United Kingdom. The savings are substantial — often 70-80% — without any compromise in technology, expertise, or outcomes. Bangalore's leading hospitals, including Manipal Hospital, Apollo Hospitals, Fortis Hospital, and Narayana Health, are equipped with the latest lithotripsy machines, flexible ureteroscopes, and laser systems for precise, minimally invasive stone removal.

There are several ways to treat kidney stones, and the right approach depends on the size, location, and composition of your stone. Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy (ESWL) uses shock waves to break stones into small fragments that pass naturally in your urine. Ureteroscopy (URS) involves passing a thin scope through the urethra and bladder to reach the stone, which is then broken with a laser. Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is used for large kidney stones and involves making a small incision in your back to access the kidney directly. Retrograde Intrarenal Surgery (RIRS) uses a flexible scope to reach stones inside the kidney without any incision.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

Kidney stone treatment is for anyone with a stone that cannot pass on its own or is causing complications. You may need treatment if your stone is larger than 6-7 mm, as stones of this size have a low chance of passing spontaneously. Stones that are causing severe pain (renal colic), blocking the urinary tract, or associated with infection require prompt intervention.

You may also need treatment if you have recurrent kidney stones. Some people form stones repeatedly due to underlying metabolic conditions, genetic factors, or chronic dehydration. In these cases, treatment involves not only removing the existing stones but also conducting a metabolic evaluation to identify the cause and implementing a prevention strategy to reduce future stone formation.

Patients with stones that have caused kidney damage — such as hydronephrosis (swelling of the kidney due to blocked urine), reduced kidney function, or chronic infection — need urgent treatment to preserve kidney health. Left untreated, obstructing stones can lead to permanent kidney damage or life-threatening sepsis.

Your urologist will determine the best treatment based on a CT scan (the gold standard for stone imaging), which shows the exact size, location, and density of your stone. The choice of procedure also depends on your anatomy, previous surgeries, and overall health. Some patients with very small, asymptomatic stones may simply be monitored, while others need immediate intervention.

## Pre-operative Preparation

Before your kidney stone treatment, your urologist will conduct a thorough evaluation. If you are travelling to Bangalore, share your medical records in advance — including any previous CT scans, ultrasound reports, blood test results, and a list of current medications. This allows the urology team to plan your procedure before you arrive.

A non-contrast CT scan (CT KUB) is the most accurate way to assess kidney stones. If you have not had one recently, it will be done in Bangalore. Blood tests including kidney function (creatinine, urea, electrolytes), complete blood count, and coagulation profile are required. A urine test checks for infection, and if present, it is treated with antibiotics before any procedure.

If you are on blood thinners — such as warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel, or newer anticoagulants — these may need to be stopped several days before your procedure to reduce bleeding risk. Your urologist will advise on the exact timing and whether you need alternative medication. Inform your doctor about all medications, supplements, and herbal remedies you take, as some can affect bleeding or anaesthesia.

For procedures under general anaesthesia (PCNL, URS, RIRS), you will need to fast for 6-8 hours before the procedure. ESWL is usually done under sedation or light anaesthesia, and fasting requirements are similar. Arrange for a family member or companion to be with you, as you will not be able to drive or travel alone immediately after the procedure.

If you have a urinary tract infection, it must be treated with a course of antibiotics before the stone procedure. Operating on an infected urinary system can spread bacteria into the bloodstream and cause sepsis, a life-threatening complication. Your urine culture results will guide the choice of antibiotic.

## Procedure Details

Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy (ESWL) is the least invasive kidney stone treatment. You lie on a special table, and a machine called a lithotripter generates focused shock waves that pass through your skin and body tissues to break the stone into small fragments. The fragments then pass naturally in your urine over the following days. ESWL is best suited for stones under 2 cm located in the kidney or upper ureter. The procedure takes 30-60 minutes, is performed under sedation, and you go home the same day. Not all stones are suitable for ESWL — very hard stones (like calcium oxalate monohydrate) may not break, and stones in the lower pole of the kidney may not clear well.

Ureteroscopy (URS) involves passing a thin, rigid or semi-rigid telescope (ureteroscope) through your urethra and bladder into the ureter to reach the stone. Once the stone is visualised, a laser (usually holmium laser) is used to fragment it into dust or small pieces. A stent (a thin plastic tube) may be left in the ureter for a few days to allow healing and ensure urine flows freely. URS is particularly effective for stones in the lower and middle ureter. The procedure takes 30-90 minutes under general or spinal anaesthesia, and most patients go home the same day or after an overnight stay.

Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is the gold standard for large kidney stones (over 2 cm) and complex stones like staghorn calculi. The surgeon makes a small incision (about 1 cm) in your back and creates a track directly into the kidney. A nephroscope is passed through this track, and the stone is broken with ultrasound, pneumatic, or laser energy and removed in pieces. PCNL has the highest stone-free rate for large stones — over 90% for single stones. The procedure takes 1-3 hours under general anaesthesia and requires a hospital stay of 2-3 days. A nephrostomy tube may be left in the kidney for a day or two to drain urine and blood.

Retrograde Intrarenal Surgery (RIRS) is a newer, minimally invasive technique for stones inside the kidney. A flexible ureteroscope is passed through the urethra, bladder, and ureter up into the kidney. A laser fibre is used to dust the stone into tiny fragments, which either pass naturally or are removed with a basket. RIRS is ideal for stones 1-2 cm in size, especially in patients who are not suitable for ESWL or PCNL. No incision is needed, recovery is faster than PCNL, and the hospital stay is typically just one day.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the day of your procedure, you will be admitted and prepared in the pre-operative area. Your vital signs are checked, an IV line is placed for fluids and medications, and the anaesthetist reviews your history. You will change into a hospital gown and be taken to the procedure room or operating theatre.

For ESWL, you lie on a water-filled cushion or table. The lithotripter uses X-ray or ultrasound to locate your stone precisely. Shock waves are then delivered — you will feel a tapping sensation on your skin, but sedation keeps you comfortable. The machine delivers 2,000-4,000 shock waves over 30-60 minutes. The urologist monitors the stone on a screen throughout and adjusts the focus as needed.

For URS or RIRS, you are given general or spinal anaesthesia. Once you are asleep or numb, the urologist inserts the ureteroscope through your urethra. A continuous flow of saline keeps the view clear. The stone is located and fragmented with a laser. If a stent is needed, it is placed at the end of the procedure. The entire process is visualised on a monitor, and X-ray imaging may be used to confirm stone clearance.

For PCNL, you are placed under general anaesthesia, usually in a prone position (face down). The urologist uses ultrasound to map the kidney and determine the best puncture site. A needle is passed through the skin into the kidney, and the track is dilated to allow the nephroscope to enter. The stone is fragmented and removed. A nephrostomy tube or stent may be placed. The procedure takes 1-3 hours depending on stone size and complexity.

After any procedure, you are taken to a recovery area where nurses monitor your vital signs, pain level, and urine output. You will be encouraged to drink plenty of fluids to help flush out stone fragments. Once you are stable and comfortable, you are transferred to your room or discharged home.

## Post-operative Care & Recovery

After kidney stone treatment, it is normal to see some blood in your urine for a few days. This is expected as the urinary tract heals. Drink plenty of water — at least 2-3 litres per day — to flush out remaining fragments and prevent new stone formation. You may experience mild pain or cramping as fragments pass, and your doctor will prescribe pain medication and possibly an alpha-blocker (like tamsulosin) to relax the ureter and ease fragment passage.

If a ureteral stent was placed, you may feel a constant urge to urinate, some discomfort in the bladder area, or pain in the flank or groin. This is normal and resolves once the stent is removed, typically 1-2 weeks after the procedure. Stent removal is a quick outpatient procedure that takes only a few minutes and does not require anaesthesia.

For PCNL patients, the recovery is slightly longer. The nephrostomy tube is usually removed after 1-2 days once the urine clears. You may have some pain at the incision site, which is managed with oral pain medication. Avoid heavy lifting and strenuous activity for 1-2 weeks. Most PCNL patients return to normal activities within two weeks.

Watch for signs of complications. Contact your doctor immediately if you develop high fever, severe pain not relieved by medication, inability to urinate, or heavy bleeding with clots. These could indicate infection, obstruction, or bleeding that needs urgent attention.

A follow-up imaging study — usually an X-ray or ultrasound — is done 1-2 weeks after treatment to confirm complete stone clearance. If any fragments remain, your urologist will discuss whether a second procedure is needed.

## Long-term Outcomes & Success Rates

The success of kidney stone treatment depends on the procedure used and the characteristics of the stone. ESWL achieves stone-free rates of 70-85% for stones under 1 cm in favourable locations. URS has a success rate of 90-95% for ureteric stones. PCNL offers the highest clearance rate — over 90% for large kidney stones — and is the standard for stones over 2 cm. RIRS achieves 85-90% clearance for stones 1-2 cm inside the kidney.

However, treating the current stone is only part of the solution. Without preventive measures, about 50% of stone formers will develop another stone within 5-10 years. This is why a metabolic evaluation is important for recurrent stone formers. This includes a 24-hour urine collection to measure volume, calcium, oxalate, uric acid, citrate, and sodium, along with blood tests for calcium, uric acid, and parathyroid hormone. Stone analysis (if you pass or have a stone removed) reveals its composition, which guides prevention.

Prevention strategies are tailored to your specific risk factors. General recommendations include drinking enough fluids to produce at least 2 litres of urine per day, reducing sodium intake, maintaining normal calcium intake (rather than restricting it), and moderating animal protein. Specific conditions may require medications — such as thiazide diuretics for calcium stones, allopurinol for uric acid stones, or potassium citrate for low citrate levels.

Bangalore's urology teams provide comprehensive stone prevention programmes, including dietary counselling, metabolic evaluation, and long-term follow-up. This holistic approach addresses not just the immediate stone but the underlying factors that cause stones to form, reducing the likelihood of recurrence and protecting your kidney function over the long term.

## Follow-up Care After Returning Home

Before you leave Bangalore, you will receive a complete treatment summary including the procedure performed, stone composition (if analysed), imaging results, and medication list. Share this with your urologist or primary care doctor at home, who will continue your follow-up care.

If a stent was placed and is due for removal, arrange this with your local urologist. Do not leave a stent in place longer than recommended, as it can encrust, cause infection, or become difficult to remove. Your Bangalore team will provide the exact timeframe for stent removal.

Drink plenty of fluids consistently — aim for 2.5-3 litres per day. This is the single most effective way to prevent new stones. Monitor your urine colour; it should be light yellow or clear. If it is dark, you need to drink more. Follow the dietary advice provided by your Bangalore team, and if a metabolic evaluation was done, discuss the results with your home urologist to implement a long-term prevention plan.

Schedule a follow-up imaging study (ultrasound or X-ray) 3-6 months after treatment to check for new stones or residual fragments. Annual imaging is recommended thereafter for recurrent stone formers. If you experience symptoms of a new stone — severe flank pain, blood in urine, or pain on urination — seek medical attention promptly.

## Alternatives to Consider

For small stones (under 5-6 mm) that are not causing severe symptoms, conservative management with pain medication and aggressive hydration may allow the stone to pass naturally. About 80% of stones under 5 mm pass without intervention. Your urologist may prescribe an alpha-blocker to relax the ureter and facilitate passage. This approach avoids surgery but requires close monitoring and may involve several days of pain.

If you have a single small stone that is not causing symptoms and is not obstructing the kidney, your doctor may recommend observation with periodic imaging rather than immediate treatment. Many small kidney stones remain stable for years and never cause problems. However, if the stone grows or starts causing symptoms, treatment should be pursued.

For patients with underlying metabolic conditions causing recurrent stones — such as hyperparathyroidism (which causes calcium stones), gout (which causes uric acid stones), or renal tubular acidosis — treating the underlying condition is essential. Parathyroid surgery may cure calcium stone formation in patients with primary hyperparathyroidism. Alkalinisation of urine with potassium citrate can dissolve uric acid stones and prevent new ones.

Dietary and lifestyle modifications form the foundation of stone prevention regardless of whether you have had a procedure. Adequate hydration, reduced salt intake, normal calcium intake, moderate animal protein, and avoidance of high-oxalate foods (if you form calcium oxalate stones) can significantly reduce recurrence. Your urologist and dietitian will provide a personalised plan based on your stone type and metabolic evaluation.

## Travel & Visa

You will need a Medical Visa (e-Medical Visa) to travel to India for kidney stone treatment. The hospital will provide a medical visa invitation letter once your treatment plan is confirmed. The visa is typically valid for up to one year with triple entry, which is useful if you need to return for follow-up or a second procedure. Family members or attendants can apply for Medical Attendant visas.

Plan to arrive in Bangalore at least two days before your scheduled procedure. This allows time for your urologist to review your records, conduct a CT scan and blood tests, assess your stone, and finalise the treatment plan. If you have an active urinary tract infection, additional time will be needed for antibiotic treatment before the procedure.

Pack comfortable, loose-fitting clothes. Bring all your medical records in an organised folder, including previous imaging studies (CT scans, ultrasounds), blood test results, and a complete list of your current medications. If you have passed a stone previously and kept it, bring it for laboratory analysis — this helps determine your stone type and guides prevention. Carry enough of your regular medications to last your entire stay.

After your procedure, plan to stay in Bangalore for 3-5 days for recovery and a follow-up assessment. ESWL patients can usually travel within 2-3 days, while PCNL patients may need 5-7 days before flying. Your urologist will clear you for travel once they are satisfied with your recovery. The hospital's international patient team will arrange your airport pickup, help with local transport, and assist with finding accommodation near the hospital for the duration of your stay.

Most surgeons recommend waiting at least 7-14 days before taking a long-haul flight, to reduce the risk of deep vein thrombosis and other travel-related complications.
