## Overview

If your kidneys can no longer filter waste products and excess fluid from your blood, dialysis becomes a lifeline — a treatment that does the work your kidneys can no longer do. Whether you are facing end-stage renal disease (ESRD), acute kidney injury, or you are waiting for a kidney transplant, dialysis keeps you alive and functioning. But the cost of dialysis in many countries is staggering, often running into hundreds of dollars per session, which can quickly become an unbearable financial burden for patients and families.

Dialysis in India offers a dramatically more affordable option without compromising on quality. A single haemodialysis session in Bangalore costs between $50 and $150, compared to $300-$500 in the United States or $200-$350 in the United Kingdom. Over weeks and months, these savings add up to tens of thousands of dollars. Bangalore's leading hospitals — including Manipal Hospital, Apollo Hospitals, Fortis Hospital, and Narayana Health — operate dedicated nephrology and dialysis units with state-of-the-art machines, trained dialysis nurses, and experienced nephrologists who supervise every session.

There are two main types of dialysis. Haemodialysis (HD) filters your blood through an artificial kidney called a dialyser, which is connected to a machine. This is done at a hospital or clinic, typically three times a week, with each session lasting about four hours. Peritoneal dialysis (PD) uses the lining of your own abdomen as a filter. A catheter is placed in your belly, and a special fluid is introduced to absorb waste products, which are then drained out. PD can be done at home, giving you more flexibility and independence.

For international patients, Bangalore offers an excellent combination of affordability, quality, and convenience. Many patients come for a period of stabilisation — getting their dialysis prescription optimised, having a vascular access created or repaired, or receiving training for home-based peritoneal dialysis — before returning to their home country for ongoing treatment. Others come for dialysis while awaiting a kidney transplant. Whatever your situation, the nephrology teams in Bangalore are experienced in managing international patients and ensuring continuity of care with your home-country doctors.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

Dialysis is for people whose kidneys can no longer adequately filter waste and fluid from the blood. This includes patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) — the final stage of chronic kidney disease — where kidney function has dropped below 10-15% of normal. Common causes include diabetes, high blood pressure, chronic glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, and autoimmune conditions.

You may also need dialysis if you have acute kidney injury (AKI), a sudden loss of kidney function that may be temporary. In these cases, dialysis supports your body while your kidneys recover, and treatment can often be stopped once kidney function returns.

If you are on the waiting list for a kidney transplant, dialysis serves as a bridge — keeping you healthy and stable until a suitable donor kidney becomes available. Many international patients come to Bangalore for dialysis while preparing for a living-donor transplant, using the time to complete evaluations and donor workup.

Your nephrologist will help determine which type of dialysis is best for you. Haemodialysis is generally preferred for patients who have good vascular access, can travel to a clinic regularly, and prefer the social interaction and supervised environment. Peritoneal dialysis suits patients who want more independence, prefer home-based treatment, and can manage the daily process themselves or with a family member's help.

## Pre-operative Preparation

Preparing for dialysis starts with a thorough assessment by a nephrologist. Before you travel to Bangalore, share your complete medical records — including recent blood tests (creatinine, urea, electrolytes, haemoglobin), kidney function estimates (eGFR), imaging studies, and any previous dialysis prescriptions. This allows the nephrology team to plan your treatment before you arrive.

If you are starting haemodialysis for the first time, you will need vascular access — a way to access your bloodstream for each session. The ideal option is an arteriovenous (AV) fistula, created surgically by connecting an artery to a vein, usually in your forearm. A fistula takes 6-12 weeks to mature before it can be used, so if you need dialysis urgently, a temporary catheter may be placed in your neck or chest vein. If you already have a working fistula or graft, bring details of its location, creation date, and any issues you have experienced.

For peritoneal dialysis, a catheter is surgically placed into your abdomen. This requires a minor procedure under local or general anaesthesia, and the catheter site needs about two weeks to heal before you can begin PD training.

Your medications will be reviewed and adjusted. Blood pressure medications may need timing changes around dialysis sessions. If you are on blood thinners, your nephrologist will advise on management. You should also ensure your vaccinations are up to date, particularly hepatitis B, as dialysis patients are at higher risk of blood-borne infections.

## Procedure Details

Haemodialysis is performed in a dedicated dialysis unit within the hospital. You will be seated in a comfortable reclining chair next to the dialysis machine. Two needles are inserted into your vascular access — one carries blood out to the dialyser, and the other returns cleaned blood to your body. The dialyser, or artificial kidney, contains a semi-permeable membrane that filters waste products and excess fluid from your blood. The machine continuously monitors blood flow, pressure, and composition throughout the session.

Each haemodialysis session lasts approximately four hours, and most patients need three sessions per week. During the session, you can read, watch television, listen to music, or even work on a laptop. A trained dialysis nurse remains present throughout, and a nephrologist is always available on-site. High-flux dialysers are used in Bangalore's leading hospitals, which provide more efficient clearance of toxins compared to conventional low-flux dialysers.

Peritoneal dialysis works differently. A soft catheter is permanently placed in your abdomen through a minor surgical procedure. During each exchange, you introduce a dialysate solution (usually 2 litres) into your abdomen through the catheter. The solution dwells inside for several hours, during which waste products and excess fluid pass from your blood vessels through the peritoneal membrane into the solution. You then drain the used solution and replace it with fresh solution. Continuous Ambulatory Peritoneal Dialysis (CAPD) involves 3-4 manual exchanges during the day. Automated Peritoneal Dialysis (APD) uses a machine called a cycler to perform exchanges overnight while you sleep.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the day of your haemodialysis session, you will arrive at the dialysis unit and be weighed to determine how much excess fluid needs to be removed. The nurse will check your blood pressure and temperature, then clean your access site and insert the two needles. You may feel a brief sting when the needles go in, but the rest of the session is painless.

Blood is drawn out through the first needle, passes through the dialyser where it is cleaned and excess fluid is removed, and returns to your body through the second needle. The machine controls the flow rate, typically 200-400 ml per minute. Throughout the session, the machine monitors your blood pressure, and the nurse checks on you regularly. If your blood pressure drops — a common side effect of fluid removal — the nurse can adjust the machine or give you fluids.

After about four hours, the session is complete. The needles are removed, and pressure is applied to the access sites to stop bleeding. You will be weighed again to confirm the target fluid has been removed. Your blood pressure is checked, and once you are stable, you can leave. Most patients feel somewhat tired after a session but recover within a few hours.

For peritoneal dialysis, the process is simpler and done at home. Each exchange takes about 30-40 minutes — 10 minutes to drain the used fluid, 10 minutes to introduce fresh fluid, and the rest is dwell time. You are free to go about your day between exchanges. APD patients simply connect to the cycler at bedtime and disconnect in the morning.

## Post-operative Care & Recovery

After each haemodialysis session, it is normal to feel some fatigue. This is partly because of the rapid fluid and electrolyte shifts that occur during treatment. Most patients recover within a few hours and can resume normal activities the same day. Some people experience low blood pressure, muscle cramps, or headaches during or after a session — these are usually managed by adjusting the dialysis prescription, slowing the fluid removal rate, or changing the dialysate composition.

Your access site requires daily care. Wash the area with soap and water, and keep it clean and dry. Never let anyone draw blood or take blood pressure readings on your access arm. Check the thrill (a buzzing sensation) in your fistula daily — if it disappears, contact the dialysis unit immediately, as it may indicate a clot. For catheter-based access, keep the dressing dry and watch for signs of infection such as redness, swelling, or discharge.

Between sessions, you need to follow a kidney-friendly diet. Limit your fluid intake to what your nephrologist recommends — typically 500-1000 ml per day plus the volume of your urine output if you still produce some. Restrict salt, potassium (found in bananas, oranges, potatoes, and tomatoes), and phosphorus (found in dairy, nuts, and colas). Eat adequate protein, as dialysis removes some protein from your blood. A dietitian will work with you to create a meal plan that fits your cultural preferences and nutritional needs.

## Long-term Outcomes & Success Rates

Dialysis is not a cure for kidney disease, but it is a highly effective life-sustaining treatment. With adequate dialysis — defined as achieving a Kt/V of at least 1.2 per session — patients can live for many years on dialysis, with some surviving 10-20 years or more. The quality of dialysis matters enormously, and Bangalore's leading hospitals achieve international standards of dialysis adequacy.

However, dialysis does not fully replicate kidney function. It does not produce erythropoietin (the hormone that stimulates red blood cell production) or activate vitamin D for bone health. This means you will need supplemental medications — erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) for anaemia, phosphate binders to control phosphorus levels, and active vitamin D analogues for bone health. These medications are an ongoing cost that should be budgeted separately from the dialysis session charges.

Long-term dialysis patients face an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, bone disease (renal osteodystrophy), and infections. Regular monitoring — including monthly blood tests, quarterly adequacy checks, and annual cardiovascular assessments — is essential. The nephrology team in Bangalore will provide a comprehensive treatment plan and coordinate with your home-country nephrologist for ongoing care.

For many patients, dialysis is a bridge to kidney transplant, which offers superior quality of life and long-term survival. If you are a transplant candidate, the Bangalore team can coordinate your dialysis with your transplant evaluation and surgery planning.

## Follow-up Care After Returning Home

If you come to Bangalore for a period of dialysis, you will receive a complete treatment summary before you leave, including your dialysis prescription, blood test results, vascular access details, and medication list. Share this with your home-country nephrologist, who will continue your ongoing dialysis care.

If you are being trained for peritoneal dialysis, you will leave with a detailed training manual, a supply of dialysate solutions, and contact information for the Bangalore PD team. Most PD patients manage independently after 1-2 weeks of training. The Bangalore team remains available for remote consultations if you encounter problems.

Continue taking all prescribed medications exactly as directed. Never skip a dialysis session — missed sessions lead to fluid overload, toxin accumulation, and potentially life-threatening complications. Monitor your weight daily, and report any sudden weight gain (which may indicate fluid retention) to your nephrologist. Watch for signs of access infection, peritonitis (in PD patients), or other complications, and seek medical attention promptly.

## Alternatives to Consider

Kidney transplant is the best alternative to long-term dialysis for eligible patients. A successful transplant offers better quality of life, fewer dietary restrictions, and improved long-term survival compared to dialysis. If you have a compatible living donor, this is generally the preferred option. Talk to your nephrologist about whether you are a transplant candidate.

Conservative kidney management — without dialysis or transplant — may be appropriate for very elderly patients or those with severe comorbidities where the burden of dialysis outweighs the benefits. This focuses on symptom control, blood pressure management, and quality of life rather than renal replacement therapy. Your nephrologist can help you understand this option.

If you have early-stage chronic kidney disease (Stage 3-4), aggressive management of underlying conditions — diabetes, blood pressure, and proteinuria — can slow disease progression and delay the need for dialysis. Medications such as ACE inhibitors or ARBs, SGLT2 inhibitors, and lifestyle changes including a kidney-friendly diet and smoking cessation can preserve kidney function for years.

## Travel & Visa

You will need a Medical Visa (e-Medical Visa) to travel to India for dialysis treatment. The hospital will provide a medical visa invitation letter once your treatment plan is confirmed. The visa is typically valid for up to one year with triple entry, which is convenient if you need to return periodically for treatment. Family members or attendants can apply for Medical Attendant visas.

Plan to arrive in Bangalore at least two to three days before your first scheduled dialysis session. This allows time for your nephrologist to review your records, conduct blood tests, assess your vascular access, and finalise your dialysis prescription. If you need a new vascular access or a peritoneal dialysis catheter, additional time will be required for the procedure and healing.

Pack comfortable, loose-fitting clothes, especially if you have an AV fistula in your arm — wear short or loose sleeves. Bring all your medical records in an organised folder, including recent blood tests, dialysis prescriptions, vascular access details, and a list of all medications. Carry enough of your regular medications (EPO, phosphate binders, vitamin D analogues) to last your entire stay, as specific brands may not be available locally.

The hospital's international patient team will arrange your airport pickup, help with local transport, and assist with finding accommodation near the hospital for the duration of your treatment. Many hospitals offer discounted rates at nearby partner hotels for dialysis patients and their families.

Most surgeons recommend waiting at least 7-14 days before taking a long-haul flight, to reduce the risk of deep vein thrombosis and other travel-related complications.
