## Overview

If you are living with a joint that locks, clicks, gives way, or aches every time you move, you already know how much it can steal from your daily life. Simple things—walking up stairs, reaching overhead, sleeping without pain—can become a struggle. Arthroscopy, often called keyhole surgery, is a minimally invasive way to look directly inside your joint and fix the problem without a large cut. Because the surgeon works through incisions just a few millimetres wide, you experience far less tissue damage, smaller scars, less pain, and a noticeably faster return to the activities you love compared with traditional open surgery.

During the procedure, your surgeon inserts a thin tube called an arthroscope through one keyhole incision. This tube carries a high-definition camera and light source that magnifies the inside of your joint onto a monitor. Through two or three additional small portals, the surgeon passes miniature instruments to trim, repair, or reconstruct damaged tissue. Arthroscopy is commonly used on your knee (for meniscal tears, ACL reconstruction, and cartilage damage), your shoulder (for rotator cuff and labral tears), your hip (for impingement and labral problems), and your ankle.

Many of these surgeons have trained at renowned sports medicine institutes in the US, UK, and Australia and have treated professional and national-team athletes. ## Who Is This Procedure For?

Arthroscopy may be right for you if you have joint pain, locking, clicking, or instability that has not responded to conservative treatments such as medication, physiotherapy, or injections. Common reasons include meniscal tears, ACL or PCL tears, cartilage damage, loose bodies within the joint, and synovial pathology. If your MRI has confirmed a structural problem inside your joint and conservative management has not given you relief, arthroscopic intervention may be your appropriate next step.

If you have shoulder problems—rotator cuff tears, labral (SLAP) tears, or impingement syndrome that limits your overhead activities and disrupts your sleep—you are also a strong candidate. Hip arthroscopy suits you if you have femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) or labral tears confirmed on MRI. Ankle arthroscopy can address impingement, loose bodies, and cartilage damage if you are an active individual.

You may be a suitable candidate if you experience mechanical symptoms such as your joint locking, catching, or giving way, or if you have persistent swelling and pain that limits your daily activities or athletic performance. You should be in reasonably good general health to tolerate anaesthesia and take part in post-operative rehabilitation. Active infections, severe cardiovascular disease, or uncontrolled diabetes may need to be addressed before your surgery.

## Pre-Operative Preparation

Once your surgery date is confirmed, you will receive a detailed pre-operative checklist from Khan Meditour and your surgical team. This typically includes blood tests (complete blood count, coagulation profile, blood group), an ECG, and dedicated imaging of your affected joint—X-rays and an MRI are standard, and a CT scan may be requested for complex cases. If you have existing medical conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, or heart disease, you may need additional clearance from your treating physician.

In the weeks leading up to your surgery, focus on optimising your physical condition. Maintaining or improving the muscle strength around your affected joint can significantly speed up your recovery. If you smoke, it is strongly advised to stop at least four weeks before surgery, because smoking impairs wound healing and increases your risk of infection and blood clots. For knee and shoulder arthroscopy, your physiotherapist may recommend pre-habilitation exercises to maintain your range of motion and muscle activation.

Certain medications must be reviewed before surgery. Blood-thinning medications such as aspirin, clopidogrel, or NSAIDs may need to be stopped 5-7 days before surgery to reduce your bleeding risk. Herbal supplements such as garlic, ginkgo biloba, fish oil, and vitamin E can also increase bleeding and should be discontinued. Always consult your surgeon before stopping any prescribed medication. On the day before surgery, you will be asked to fast for at least 6-8 hours.

## Procedure Details

Your surgery is performed under spinal, regional, or general anaesthesia depending on which joint is being treated and what needs fixing. Your surgeon makes two to four small incisions (portals) around the joint, each typically 5-8 millimetres long. The arthroscope goes in through one portal, and your joint is filled with sterile saline fluid to expand the space and give a clear, magnified view of every internal structure on the monitor.

Through the other portals, your surgeon introduces specialised instruments—shavers, graspers, scissors, and radiofrequency probes. Depending on your diagnosis, your surgeon may trim or repair a torn meniscus, remove loose bodies, smooth damaged cartilage, reconstruct a torn ACL or PCL, repair a rotator cuff tear, or decompress shoulder impingement. For ligament reconstructions, a tendon graft—often taken from your own hamstring or patellar tendon—is prepared and fixed in place with bioabsorbable or metallic screws. The entire procedure usually takes 30-90 minutes, depending on complexity.

After surgery, you begin moving on the same day or the next morning. A hinged brace or sling may support the joint initially. Most people go home within 24 hours carrying a structured physiotherapy programme. Your recovery time ranges from about 2 weeks for simple debridement to 8 weeks for ligament reconstruction, with a full return to sports typically at 6-9 months after ACL reconstruction.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the morning of your surgery, you will be admitted and taken to the pre-operative holding area. Your anaesthesiologist will place an intravenous line and administer the chosen anaesthetic. For knee arthroscopy, a spinal block is commonly used. For shoulder arthroscopy, a regional nerve block is often combined with general anaesthesia for extended pain relief.

Once you are anaesthetised, the surgical team positions your joint, prepares your skin with antiseptic, and your surgeon makes the small portal incisions. The arthroscope is inserted and your joint is distended with sterile saline for a clear, magnified view. Your surgeon systematically inspects every compartment, then performs the necessary repairs—trimming a torn meniscus, reconstructing an ACL with a tendon graft, or reattaching a rotator cuff using suture anchors.

Once complete, the saline is drained, portals are closed with sutures or adhesive strips, and a sterile dressing is applied. A brace or sling may be fitted. The entire operation typically takes 30-90 minutes, and you will be transferred to the recovery room as the anaesthetic wears off.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

In the immediate post-operative period, you will be monitored in the recovery room until your anaesthetic has worn off and your vital signs are stable. Your pain management is tailored to your procedure, typically combining the regional nerve block with oral medications such as paracetamol and NSAIDs. This multimodal approach keeps your pain well-controlled while minimising side effects.

Your mobilisation begins on the day of surgery or the following morning. A physiotherapist will guide you through initial exercises to reduce swelling, restore your range of motion, and activate the muscles around your joint. For knee arthroscopy, you may walk with crutches or a brace for the first few days. For shoulder arthroscopy, your arm will be in a sling and passive exercises will begin immediately. Most people are discharged within 24 hours with a detailed home exercise programme.

After discharge, you will continue supervised physiotherapy at your accommodation or an outpatient clinic in Bangalore for 1-2 weeks. The focus shifts to progressive strengthening, proprioception training, and functional activities. A final wound review is performed before you receive your medical fitness-to-fly certificate, typically 3-7 days after surgery for simple procedures and 7-10 days for more complex reconstructions.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

Arthroscopy has consistently high success rates across a range of joint conditions. For meniscal trimming, over 85% of people report significant pain relief and return to normal activities within 2-4 weeks. ACL reconstruction has a success rate of 90-95%, with most people returning to their pre-injury level of sport within 6-9 months when they adhere to a structured rehabilitation protocol. Rotator cuff repair outcomes depend on tear size and tissue quality, with satisfaction rates of 85-90% for small to medium tears.

Your long-term outcome depends on several factors, including the nature of your original injury, the quality of the repair, and your commitment to rehabilitation. For ligament reconstructions, your graft continues to heal and mature for up to 12 months, and return-to-sport testing is used to ensure you meet objective criteria before resuming competitive activity. Those who complete their full rehabilitation programme consistently achieve better outcomes than those who stop prematurely. No procedure can guarantee a perfect result, but disciplined rehabilitation gives you the best possible chance.

## Alternatives to Consider

Before committing to arthroscopic surgery, it is worth exploring conservative alternatives, particularly if your symptoms are mild or moderate. These include activity modification, physiotherapy to strengthen the muscles supporting your joint, and analgesic or anti-inflammatory medications. Intra-articular injections of corticosteroids can provide temporary relief of inflammation, while viscosupplementation (hyaluronic acid injections) may lubricate your joint and reduce pain for several months. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections are increasingly used for tendon and mild cartilage conditions.

For certain conditions, bracing or orthotic devices may give you symptomatic relief, particularly if you are not a surgical candidate or wish to delay surgery. A functional knee brace can stabilise an ACL-deficient knee if your demands are low.

However, when structural damage is confirmed on your imaging and conservative management has failed to give you adequate relief, arthroscopy remains the most effective minimally invasive solution. For advanced arthritis with diffuse cartilage loss, joint replacement rather than arthroscopy may be the more appropriate surgical option. Your surgeon will recommend the best approach based on your MRI findings, clinical examination, and your individual circumstances.
