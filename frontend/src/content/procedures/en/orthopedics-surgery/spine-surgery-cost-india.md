## Overview

If you are reading this, you have probably been living with back or leg pain for months — perhaps years — and conservative treatments like physiotherapy, medications and injections have stopped helping. You may be finding it difficult to walk, to sleep, to sit through a workday, or to enjoy time with your family. You may also be experiencing numbness, tingling or weakness in your legs, which can be frightening. The decision to have spine surgery is never easy, but for the right patient, it can be life-changing — relieving pain, preserving nerve function and restoring independence.

Spine surgery encompasses a wide range of procedures, from minimally invasive microdiscectomy for a herniated disc to complex spinal fusion for degenerative instability or scoliosis correction. The goal is to relieve your pain, preserve or restore your neurological function, and stabilise your spinal column. Surgery is considered only when conservative treatments have failed to provide adequate relief.

In Bangalore, spine surgery is performed at JCI-accredited hospitals by fellowship-trained spine surgeons — many trained at leading institutions in the US, UK and Europe — using advanced intraoperative imaging (O-arm, C-arm), neuro-monitoring and navigation systems. Both open and minimally invasive techniques are available. The total package costs 85–90% less than in the United States, and patients who face waiting lists of 12–24 months at home can often be scheduled within 1–2 weeks.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

Spine surgery may be recommended if you have exhausted conservative treatment options — typically at least 6–12 weeks of physiotherapy, medication and activity modification — and continue to experience significant pain or neurological symptoms. Common indications include a herniated or bulging disc causing persistent radiculopathy or sciatica, spinal stenosis with neurogenic claudication, and degenerative disc disease with instability or deformity such as spondylolisthesis.

You may be a suitable candidate if you experience persistent back or leg pain that interferes with sleep, limits your ability to walk, or is accompanied by neurological symptoms such as numbness, tingling, weakness, or bladder or bowel dysfunction. Progressive neurological deficit — worsening weakness or sensory loss — is a strong indication for timely surgical intervention to prevent permanent nerve damage.

If you have spinal deformities such as scoliosis or kyphosis that are progressive, painful, or causing cardiopulmonary compromise, corrective surgery may benefit you. Spinal tumours, infections or unstable fractures may require urgent surgical stabilisation. You must be in reasonably good general health to tolerate anaesthesia and rehabilitation.

## Pre-Operative Preparation

Once your surgery date is confirmed, you will receive a detailed pre-operative checklist. This typically includes blood tests, an ECG and echocardiogram to assess cardiac fitness, a chest X-ray, and dedicated spinal imaging — MRI is essential for visualising neural compression, while CT scans and dynamic (flexion-extension) X-rays help assess bony anatomy and instability.

In the weeks leading up to surgery, focus on optimising your physical condition. Maintaining or improving core strength and flexibility can speed up your recovery. If you smoke, stop at least four weeks before surgery, as smoking impairs bone healing and increases the risk of infection. Blood-thinning medications such as aspirin, clopidogrel, warfarin or NSAIDs may need to be stopped 5–7 days before surgery. Herbal supplements such as garlic, ginkgo biloba, fish oil and vitamin E can also increase bleeding and should be discontinued.

## Procedure Details

The specific technique depends on your underlying condition. For a herniated disc, microdiscectomy involves a small incision (2–3 cm), removal of a portion of the lamina, and extraction of the herniated disc fragment compressing your nerve root — all performed under microscope or endoscope magnification. For spinal stenosis, laminectomy widens your spinal canal by removing the lamina and thickened ligaments, relieving pressure on your spinal cord and nerve roots. These decompression procedures are typically completed in 60–90 minutes with minimal blood loss.

For degenerative instability or spondylolisthesis, spinal fusion is performed using pedicle screws and rods to stabilise the affected vertebrae, with bone graft placed between the vertebrae to achieve bony union. Interbody cages (PLIF, TLIF) may be used to restore disc height. For scoliosis or complex deformity, multi-level fusion with corrective osteotomies is performed, and the procedure may take 4–8 hours depending on the number of levels involved. All procedures are conducted under intraoperative neuromonitoring to minimise the risk of nerve injury.

Post-operatively, you will begin mobilisation within 24–48 hours with a brace and assistance from a physiotherapist. Most patients are discharged within 3–7 days with a structured home exercise programme. Full recovery varies from 3 months for simple decompression to 12 months for complex fusion.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the morning of your surgery, you will be admitted and taken to the pre-operative holding area. The anaesthesiologist will review your medical history and administer general anaesthesia. Intraoperative neuromonitoring electrodes will be placed on your limbs to provide real-time feedback on spinal cord and nerve root function throughout the surgery.

Once you are anaesthetised, the surgical team will position you appropriately (face-down for most lumbar procedures) and prepare the skin. For microdiscectomy, the surgeon makes a small 2–3 cm incision, uses a microscope for magnification, removes a small portion of the lamina, and extracts the herniated disc fragment. For laminectomy, a larger window is created to widen the spinal canal. For spinal fusion, the surgeon exposes the affected vertebrae, places pedicle screws under fluoroscopic or navigated guidance, and connects them with rods. Bone graft is placed between the vertebrae to achieve bony union.

The duration ranges from 60–90 minutes for simple microdiscectomy to 4–8 hours for complex fusion. You will be transferred to the recovery room or ICU, where the nursing team monitors your vital signs, neurological function and pain levels.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

**Will it hurt?** Pain management is a priority. A multimodal analgesia protocol combines intravenous medications, oral analgesics and sometimes a patient-controlled analgesia pump. You will have surgical site pain, but it is well managed and gradually subsides over the first week.

Mobilisation begins within 24–48 hours of surgery, with a physiotherapist assisting you in sitting up, standing and taking initial steps. A lumbar or cervical brace is typically worn for 6–12 weeks after fusion surgery. Over the next 3–7 days, you will progress to walking longer distances, climbing stairs and performing basic daily activities with appropriate precautions.

After discharge, you will continue supervised physiotherapy for 1–2 weeks in Bangalore. A final wound review and imaging are performed before you receive your medical fitness-to-fly certificate — typically 7–10 days after simple decompression and 14–21 days after fusion surgery. Full recovery ranges from 3 months for simple decompression to 12 months for complex fusion.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

Spine surgery has consistently high success rates. For microdiscectomy, 85–90% of patients report significant relief of leg pain (radiculopathy) and return to normal activities within 4–6 weeks. Laminectomy for spinal stenosis has satisfaction rates of 80–85%, with most patients experiencing improved walking distance. Spinal fusion outcomes depend on the underlying pathology and number of levels fused, with successful fusion achieved in 90–95% of single-level procedures.

Long-term outcomes depend on the nature of your condition, the quality of the surgical correction, your commitment to rehabilitation, and lifestyle factors such as smoking and body weight. For fusion patients, bone graft healing continues for 6–12 months, and activity restrictions are gradually lifted as imaging confirms solid fusion.

## Follow-Up Care After Returning Home

After returning home, your recovery continues under the guidance of a local physiotherapist. Khan Meditour will provide you with a detailed discharge summary, surgical notes, implant details and a physiotherapy protocol. Continue physiotherapy for at least 6–12 weeks, progressing from gentle mobility and core exercises to strengthening and functional activities.

If you experience new or worsening neurological symptoms — weakness, numbness, bladder or bowel dysfunction — or severe back pain, fever above 38.5°C, or clear fluid drainage from the wound, contact your local doctor or your Bangalore surgeon immediately. Routine follow-up X-rays are recommended at 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and then annually for 2–3 years to monitor fusion maturation and implant position. If you had fusion surgery, avoid smoking and NSAIDs for the first 3–6 months, as both can impair bone healing.

## Alternatives to Consider

Before committing to spine surgery, you may explore conservative alternatives, particularly if your symptoms are moderate or have been present for less than 6–12 weeks. These include activity modification, physiotherapy to strengthen core and paraspinal muscles, and analgesic or anti-inflammatory medications. Epidural steroid injections or nerve root blocks can provide targeted relief of inflammatory pain.

For certain conditions, less invasive surgical options may be appropriate. Endoscopic spine surgery offers a less invasive alternative to microdiscectomy, with smaller incisions and faster recovery. Artificial disc replacement may be an alternative to fusion for carefully selected patients with single-level disc disease, preserving motion at the affected segment. Your surgeon will recommend the best approach based on your MRI findings, clinical examination and individual circumstances.
