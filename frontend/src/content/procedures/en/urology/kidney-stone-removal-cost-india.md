## Overview

If you have ever experienced the agonising pain of a kidney stone, you understand why it is often described as one of the most painful experiences a person can endure. The pain typically comes in waves, radiating from your flank to your lower abdomen and groin, and can be accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and blood in your urine. Kidney stones form when certain substances in your urine — such as calcium, oxalate, uric acid, or struvite — become concentrated and crystallise. While small stones may pass on their own with plenty of fluids, larger stones can become lodged in the kidney or ureter, causing obstruction, infection, and potentially permanent kidney damage.

When a kidney stone is too large to pass naturally — typically over 2 centimetres — or when it is causing persistent symptoms, obstruction, or infection, surgical removal becomes necessary. Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is the gold-standard surgical treatment for large kidney stones. It is a minimally invasive procedure, meaning no large open incisions are made. Your urologist creates a small keyhole incision — about 1 centimetre — in your back, through which a nephroscope is passed directly into the kidney. Using specialised instruments, the stone is fragmented and removed piece by piece. The result is a stone-free kidney without the trauma and prolonged recovery of open surgery.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

PCNL may be the right choice for you if you have a large kidney stone that is unlikely to pass on its own or break with less invasive treatments. If your stone is over 2 centimetres in diameter, if you have a staghorn calculus that fills a significant portion of your kidney's collecting system, or if you have multiple stones in one kidney, PCNL offers the highest chance of complete clearance in a single procedure.

You may also need PCNL if your stone is causing complications. These include obstruction of the urinary tract with hydronephrosis — where urine backs up into the kidney and causes it to swell — recurrent urinary tract infections, especially with stones composed of struvite (infection stones), progressive kidney damage from prolonged obstruction, or persistent pain that affects your quality of life. These are situations where surgery is not just about relieving symptoms but about protecting your kidney function.

The location and hardness of your stone matter. PCNL is most effective for stones located within the kidney itself or the upper ureter. For stones that are extremely hard — such as those composed of calcium oxalate monohydrate — PCNL is often the only technique that can reliably break and remove them. For stones in the lower pole of the kidney, where ESWL is less effective due to poor fragment clearance, PCNL is the preferred approach. Your urologist will assess your stone through a CT scan and discuss the best approach for you.

## Pre-operative Preparation

Before you travel to Bangalore, share your medical records with your urologist for advance review. This should include any recent CT scans of your abdomen and pelvis, blood tests including kidney function and coagulation profile, urine culture results, and a complete list of your medications. If you are taking blood thinners such as aspirin, clopidogrel, or warfarin, your urologist will advise you to stop them several days before surgery to reduce bleeding risk. Do not stop any medication without consulting your doctor first.

Once you arrive in Bangalore, you will undergo a comprehensive pre-operative assessment. This includes a physical examination, blood tests (complete blood count, kidney function, coagulation profile, serum calcium and uric acid), a urinalysis and urine culture to check for infection, and a non-contrast CT scan (CT KUB) to precisely map the size, location, and density of your stone. If your urine culture shows infection, you will be treated with antibiotics before surgery. You will also be assessed for any anatomical variations that might affect the percutaneous access tract. You will be asked to fast for 6 to 8 hours before the procedure.

## Procedure Details

Your PCNL is performed under general anaesthesia. You will be completely asleep and feel nothing during the procedure. The surgery typically takes 1 to 3 hours, depending on the size and number of stones and the complexity of the access tract.

The procedure begins with you positioned on your back initially. Your urologist performs a cystoscopy — passing a small telescope through your urethra and bladder into the affected kidney's ureter — and places a ureteric catheter to inject contrast dye and create a roadmap of your kidney's collecting system. You are then repositioned onto your abdomen (prone position), and your urologist uses ultrasound and X-ray guidance to identify the optimal point for the keyhole access tract through your back into the kidney.

A small incision — about 1 centimetre — is made in your skin, and a needle is passed through the back muscles and into the kidney's collecting system. Once the needle is correctly positioned, a guidewire is passed and the tract is gradually dilated using serial dilators or a balloon dilator. A working sheath is then placed, creating a tunnel through which the nephroscope can be passed directly into the kidney. Your urologist visualises the stone on a high-definition monitor and uses energy sources — such as ultrasonic, pneumatic, or laser lithotripters — to fragment the stone. The fragments are then removed through the sheath using grasping forceps or suction.

Modern Mini-PCNL technology uses smaller sheaths and scopes, which reduces bleeding and trauma to the kidney while maintaining excellent stone clearance rates. Many Bangalore hospitals now use these miniaturised systems as standard practice for appropriately sized stones. Once all the stone fragments have been removed and the kidney is confirmed to be stone-free on imaging, a nephrostomy tube is placed through the tract to drain urine and control bleeding, and a urinary catheter is inserted. In selected cases with minimal bleeding, a tubeless technique is used where the tract is sealed without a nephrostomy tube.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the day of your surgery, you will be admitted to the pre-operative area. The nursing team will start an intravenous line, give you antibiotics to prevent infection, and attach monitoring equipment. The anaesthesiologist will administer general anaesthesia, and once you are asleep, you will be positioned for the initial cystoscopy and ureteric catheter placement.

After the ureteric catheter is in place and contrast has outlined your kidney's collecting system, you are carefully turned onto your abdomen. Your urologist uses real-time ultrasound and fluoroscopic (X-ray) guidance to locate the ideal puncture site on your back. The needle is advanced into the targeted kidney calyx, and once urine or contrast confirms correct placement, the tract is dilated to accommodate the working sheath.

The nephroscope is passed through the sheath, and your urologist identifies the stone. Using the appropriate lithotripsy device, the stone is fragmented into manageable pieces. Ultrasonic lithotripsy simultaneously fragments and suctions out the pieces, while laser lithotripsy offers precise fragmentation with minimal collateral damage. The fragments are removed, and the entire collecting system is inspected to ensure no residual stones remain. A final X-ray confirms stone clearance. A nephrostomy tube and urinary catheter are placed, and you are transferred to the recovery area for monitoring as the anaesthesia wears off.

## Post-operative Care & Recovery

After your surgery, you will be transferred to the urology ward. The most important aspect of your immediate post-operative care is monitoring the nephrostomy tube drainage, which may be blood-tinged for the first 24 hours. The nursing staff will track the drainage colour and volume closely. You will likely feel some discomfort at the puncture site and from the nephrostomy tube, which is manageable with medication and resolves once the tube is removed.

You will be encouraged to drink plenty of fluids to help flush the system and reduce the risk of clot formation. Once the drainage clears and your urine output is satisfactory, the nephrostomy tube will be removed — usually on the second or third day after surgery. The urinary catheter may remain a day longer to ensure the ureter is not obstructed. You will then be observed to ensure you can urinate independently and are free of fever before being discharged.

Before you leave the hospital, your urologist will review the stone analysis if available and provide detailed instructions on activity restrictions, fluid intake, and medications. You should avoid heavy lifting, strenuous exercise, and long-distance travel for 2 to 4 weeks. It is normal to experience some flank discomfort, mild haematuria, and urinary frequency for the first few weeks as the nephrostomy tract and kidney heal.

## Long-term Outcomes & Success Rates

PCNL has excellent long-term outcomes, with stone clearance rates of 85-95% reported in most studies, depending on stone size and complexity. Bangalore's urology centres report outcomes consistent with international benchmarks, thanks to high procedural volumes and access to the latest endoscopic and lithotripsy technology. Most patients experience complete resolution of their symptoms and preservation of kidney function after successful stone removal.

The removed stone is always sent for chemical analysis, which is crucial for guiding preventive strategies. Knowing whether your stone is composed of calcium oxalate, uric acid, struvite, or cystine helps your urologist recommend targeted dietary and medical interventions to reduce your risk of forming new stones.

It is important to understand that PCNL removes the existing stones but does not cure the underlying metabolic tendency to form them. Without preventive measures, approximately 50% of patients will form a new stone within 5 to 10 years. Maintaining high fluid intake, following dietary recommendations, taking prescribed medications, and keeping regular follow-up appointments with your urologist are essential for long-term stone prevention.

## Follow-up Care After Returning Home

Before you leave Bangalore, you will receive a comprehensive discharge summary detailing your procedure, stone analysis results, medications prescribed, and follow-up recommendations. Share this document with your urologist at home, who will take over your ongoing care. A follow-up appointment is typically scheduled at 4 to 6 weeks, at which point an imaging study — such as an ultrasound or X-ray — may be performed to confirm stone clearance and assess kidney function.

You should continue to drink at least 2.5 to 3 litres of fluids daily to keep your urine dilute and reduce the risk of new stone formation. Avoid constipation, as straining can precipitate bleeding from the healing nephrostomy tract. If you have been prescribed antibiotics, complete the full course. If you were on blood thinners before surgery, your urologist will advise when it is safe to restart them.

Seek immediate medical attention if you experience severe bleeding with bright red blood or clots in your urine, inability to urinate, fever with chills, or severe flank pain. These could indicate a complication such as infection, obstruction, or bleeding that requires prompt treatment. ## Alternatives to Consider

If your stone is small (under 1.5 centimetres) and located in the upper urinary tract, Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy (ESWL) may be an effective non-invasive option. ESWL uses shock waves generated outside the body to break the stone into fragments that pass naturally in the urine. It requires no incisions or anaesthesia in some cases, though it is less effective for hard stones and those in the lower pole of the kidney.

For stones in the ureter or smaller kidney stones, Retrograde Intrarenal Surgery (RIRS) with a flexible ureteroscope and laser is an excellent option. RIRS involves passing a flexible scope through the urethra and bladder up into the kidney, with no incisions at all. It is particularly suitable for stones under 2 centimetres and for patients who cannot safely undergo PCNL.

For very large or complex staghorn stones that fill the entire kidney, open or laparoscopic stone surgery may occasionally be necessary, though this is rare with modern PCNL techniques. Your urologist will discuss which technique is most appropriate for your specific stone size, location, and kidney anatomy.
