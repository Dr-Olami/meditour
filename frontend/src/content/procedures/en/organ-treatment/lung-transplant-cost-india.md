## Overview

Being told that you need a lung transplant is one of the most overwhelming moments you can face. You may have been living with worsening breathlessness for years — perhaps you are now dependent on oxygen around the clock, unable to walk across a room, and watching your world shrink. The decision to pursue a transplant is enormous, and it is completely natural to feel frightened, hopeful, and exhausted all at once. This guide will help you understand what a lung transplant in Bangalore involves, so you and your family can face the road ahead with clarity.

A lung transplant is a life-saving surgical procedure in which one or both of your diseased lungs are replaced with healthy lungs from a donor. It is typically recommended for patients with end-stage lung disease — including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cystic fibrosis and pulmonary arterial hypertension — where other treatments have been exhausted. In India, living-donor lobar lung transplantation is the primary approach for international patients, where lower lobes from healthy relatives are surgically removed and transplanted into you.

Bangalore is home to several JCI-accredited hospitals with dedicated lung transplant units, advanced ICU infrastructure with ECMO capability, and multidisciplinary teams comprising pulmonologists, transplant surgeons, anaesthesiologists and specialised nursing staff. A lung transplant that would cost over $1,000,000 in the United States can be performed for between $40,000 and $90,000 in Bangalore — without compromising on quality of care or safety standards.

Indian organ transplant regulations require international patients to bring compatible living donors who are close relatives. Deceased-donor lungs are allocated to Indian citizens. The hospital's transplant authorisation committee, along with state government authorities, rigorously reviews every case to verify the donor-recipient relationship and confirm that the donation is voluntary and without financial coercion.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

A lung transplant is indicated if you have end-stage lung disease whose respiratory function has deteriorated to the point where maximal medical therapy and pulmonary rehabilitation can no longer sustain adequate quality of life or survival. Common conditions include COPD, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis and pulmonary arterial hypertension. If you have progressive oxygen dependence, recurrent hospitalisations for respiratory failure, or a sharply declining FEV1, you may be a candidate.

You must be medically fit enough to withstand a major surgical procedure and lifelong immunosuppressive therapy. This means having adequate cardiac function, no active infections and no uncontrolled malignancies. Age is not an absolute barrier, but older patients undergo more careful assessment. If you have a history of non-compliance, ongoing tobacco use or substance abuse, you may require additional psychosocial evaluation.

Critically, as an international patient you must bring compatible living donors who are close relatives. For a bilateral lobar transplant, two donors are typically required, each donating a lower lobe. Each donor must be in good health, aged between 18 and 55, have a compatible blood group, and undergo comprehensive medical and psychological evaluation. Both donors and you must demonstrate a realistic understanding of the risks, the lifelong commitment to immunosuppressive therapy, and the financial capacity to comply with post-transplant care.

## Pre-Operative Preparation

Preparation begins weeks before travel and involves coordination between your home-country physicians and the Bangalore transplant team. You should ensure that all recent medical records — including pulmonary function tests, imaging studies, cardiac evaluations and medication lists — are shared with the Indian hospital in advance. This allows the transplant team to review your case and request any additional tests before arrival, minimising delays.

In the weeks leading up to the transplant, you should maintain your prescribed oxygen therapy and pulmonary rehabilitation regimen to optimise your condition for surgery. Blood pressure, blood sugar and cardiac function should be well controlled. You must avoid smoking and alcohol completely. Nutritional status should be optimised, as malnutrition is common in end-stage lung disease and can impair recovery.

Blood-thinning medications are typically discontinued several days in advance. You should provide a complete list of all medications, supplements and herbal remedies to the transplant team. Vaccinations should be up to date, particularly for influenza, pneumococcal disease and COVID-19, as post-transplant immunosuppression significantly increases infection susceptibility.

## Procedure Details

The lung transplant process begins with a comprehensive evaluation of both you and your living donor(s). This includes blood tests, imaging studies, pulmonary function tests, cardiac catheterisation, tissue typing and psychosocial assessments. Once compatibility is confirmed and the transplant authorisation committee grants approval, the surgery is scheduled.

The living-donor lobar lung transplant involves simultaneous surgeries: the donor lobectomies, in which the lower lobes are removed from two healthy donors (for a bilateral transplant), and your recipient surgery, in which your diseased lungs are excised and the donor lobes are implanted. For a single-lung lobar transplant, one donor may suffice.

The surgery lasts between 6 and 10 hours under general anaesthesia. Your diseased lung(s) are carefully removed, preserving the vital blood vessels and airways, and the donor lobe(s) are connected to your vascular and bronchial systems. Cardiopulmonary bypass or ECMO may be required during the procedure to maintain circulation and oxygenation.

After surgery, you will be transferred to the ICU with mechanical ventilation support for close monitoring of lung function, oxygenation, bleeding and signs of rejection. Immunosuppressive medications are initiated immediately. Your donor(s) are monitored in separate recovery units and typically spend 5 to 7 days in hospital before discharge.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the day of surgery, you and your donor(s) are admitted early in the morning and prepared in separate pre-operative areas. The surgical teams work simultaneously to minimise the time you are under anaesthesia and the lungs are without blood flow.

The donor lobectomy begins with a careful incision to access the chest cavity. The surgical team identifies and divides the blood vessels and airways supplying the lower lobe, which is then removed and immediately flushed with a cold preservation solution. Each donor typically spends 5 to 7 days in hospital and can expect a recovery period of several weeks before resuming normal activities.

In your recipient theatre, your diseased lung(s) are mobilised and carefully removed while preserving the pulmonary arteries, pulmonary veins and main airway. The donor lobe(s) are then implanted by connecting the donor's pulmonary vessels and bronchus to your corresponding structures. Cardiopulmonary bypass or ECMO may be used during the procedure. Once blood flow is restored, the surgical team checks for bleeding and confirms that the new lung is ventilating and perfusing adequately. The entire procedure typically takes 6 to 10 hours.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

The first few days after your lung transplant are spent in the ICU, where you are continuously monitored for lung function, oxygenation, bleeding, infection and signs of rejection. Blood tests are performed daily to track blood gases, inflammatory markers and immunosuppressive drug levels. Bronchoscopy may be performed to inspect the airway anastomosis and collect samples.

**Will it hurt?** Pain management is a priority, with a combination of epidural and intravenous analgesics gradually reduced as healing progresses. You will have significant chest discomfort, but it is well managed and gradually subsides. Early mobilisation is encouraged, with physiotherapists assisting you to sit up, stand and walk within the first few days. Pulmonary rehabilitation begins early and is a cornerstone of your recovery.

As you stabilise, you will be transferred to a private room. Immunosuppressive medications are carefully titrated, and you will begin learning about your medication schedule, side effects and the critical importance of adherence. Throughout the hospital stay, the transplant team watches for complications such as primary graft dysfunction, airway dehiscence, infections and acute rejection. Most complications, if detected early, can be managed successfully. You are typically discharged after 3 to 6 weeks, depending on your recovery progress.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

Leading transplant centres in Bangalore report one-year patient survival rates of 85–90% and graft survival rates of 83–88%, comparable to international benchmarks. Five-year survival rates are typically 55–70%, reflecting the long-term challenges of immunosuppression, chronic rejection and the underlying disease process. Outcomes are influenced by your overall health, the quality of the donor lobes, and strict adherence to post-transplant medication and pulmonary rehabilitation.

For most patients, a successful lung transplant dramatically improves quality of life. Patients previously debilitated by breathlessness, oxygen dependence and recurrent hospitalisations often return to near-normal activity levels within 6 to 12 months. Many resume work, travel and family life without the constraints of continuous oxygen therapy.

However, lifelong immunosuppressive therapy carries ongoing risks, including increased susceptibility to infections, a higher incidence of certain cancers, and potential metabolic complications. Chronic rejection, known as bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome, remains the leading cause of long-term graft failure. Regular monitoring and proactive management of these risks are essential.

## Follow-Up Care After Returning Home

After returning home, you must continue close coordination with both your Bangalore transplant team and a local pulmonologist. In the first few months, blood tests and pulmonary function tests are typically required weekly to monitor lung function and immunosuppressive drug levels, with frequency gradually decreasing as stability is confirmed. The Bangalore team remains available for remote consultation.

Immunosuppressive medications must be taken exactly as prescribed, at the same times each day, without missing doses. You should carry a detailed medication list and emergency contact information at all times. You should also be vigilant about infection prevention — avoiding crowded places and sick contacts, practising meticulous hand hygiene, wearing masks in high-risk settings, and staying up to date with recommended vaccinations.

You should seek immediate medical attention if you experience fever, worsening breathlessness, reduced exercise tolerance, cough with sputum or any signs of infection, as these may indicate rejection or a serious complication.

## Alternatives to Consider

Before pursuing a lung transplant, you should explore all other treatment options with your pulmonologist. For COPD, optimisation of bronchodilator therapy, pulmonary rehabilitation, oxygen supplementation and lung volume reduction surgery (in selected cases) may improve symptoms and delay the need for transplant. For pulmonary fibrosis, antifibrotic medications such as pirfenidone or nintedanib may help. For pulmonary arterial hypertension, advanced vasodilator therapies may be effective.

If you are not yet ready for transplant or do not have suitable living donors, maximal medical management remains a viable option. While it does not reverse end-stage lung disease, it can sustain you and improve quality of life for a period. A lung transplant is generally reserved for patients whose lung disease has progressed to the point where medical therapy can no longer sustain adequate function or quality of life. The decision should be made jointly by you, your family, your pulmonologist and the transplant team.

## Travel & Visa

You will need a medical visa (e-Medical Visa), which can be applied for online. The hospital will provide a medical visa invitation letter once the evaluation and scheduling are confirmed. The medical visa is typically valid for 60 days and can be extended if a longer stay is required. Your donors and accompanying attendants should apply for medical attendant visas.

You should plan to arrive in Bangalore at least two weeks before the scheduled surgery date to complete pre-transplant evaluations, donor workup and committee approvals. It is advisable to travel with two or more attendants (including the donors) who can provide support during the hospital stay and recovery period. Ensure that all medical records, donor relationship documents and pathology reports are carried in original or attested copies — documents typically include birth certificates or family registry records proving the donor-recipient relationship, recent imaging studies, biopsy reports and a referral letter from your home-country pulmonologist. All documents should be in English or accompanied by certified translations.

Most surgeons recommend waiting at least 7-14 days before taking a long-haul flight, to reduce the risk of deep vein thrombosis and other travel-related complications.
