## Overview

If you or someone you love has been told that a liver transplant may be the only path forward, you are likely feeling overwhelmed and exhausted by the journey so far. End-stage liver disease takes a heavy toll on your body and your emotions, and the thought of a major operation in another country can feel daunting. This guide is here to walk you through what a liver transplant in Bangalore really involves, so you can make decisions with clarity rather than fear.

A liver transplant is a life-saving operation in which your failing liver is replaced with a healthy one from a donor. In India, the approach used for international patients is almost always a living-donor liver transplant, meaning a portion of a healthy relative's liver is surgically removed and placed into you. Because the liver has a remarkable ability to regenerate, both your donor's remaining liver and the transplanted portion will grow to an adequate size within several weeks, making this partial donation possible.

Bangalore has become a leading destination for this surgery, home to several JCI-accredited hospitals with dedicated liver transplant units, advanced intensive care facilities, and multidisciplinary teams. Many senior surgeons here have trained or worked in the UK, USA, and Germany, bringing decades of experience in complex hepatobiliary surgery. A liver transplant that would cost over $600,000 in the United States can be performed for between $25,000 and $60,000 in Bangalore, without compromising on quality of care or safety standards.

There is an important legal point you must understand early. Indian organ transplant regulations require international patients to bring a compatible living donor who is a close relative. Deceased-donor livers are allocated to Indian citizens. The hospital's transplant authorisation committee, along with state government authorities, will rigorously review your case to verify the donor-recipient relationship and confirm that the donation is voluntary and free of financial coercion.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

A liver transplant may be the right path for you if you have end-stage liver disease and other treatments have been exhausted. This includes decompensated cirrhosis due to hepatitis B or C, alcohol-related liver disease, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), or autoimmune conditions. If you have acute liver failure from drug toxicity or viral hepatitis and your liver does not recover spontaneously, urgent transplantation may also be necessary.

If you have certain liver cancers, particularly hepatocellular carcinoma meeting the Milan criteria, you may be a candidate for transplant when the tumour is confined to the liver and has not spread to blood vessels or distant organs.

You must be medically fit enough to withstand a major surgical procedure, with adequate cardiac and pulmonary function, no active infections, and no uncontrolled malignancies outside the liver. If you have severe heart failure, uncontrolled diabetes, or active substance abuse, you may not be a suitable candidate until those conditions are addressed.

Critically, as an international patient you must bring a compatible living donor who is a close relative, as per Indian organ transplant regulations. Your donor must be in good health, aged between 18 and 55, have a compatible blood group, and undergo comprehensive medical and psychological evaluation. Both you and your donor must demonstrate a realistic understanding of the risks, the lifelong commitment to immunosuppressive therapy, and the financial capacity to comply with post-transplant care.

## Pre-Operative Preparation

Your preparation begins weeks before you travel and involves coordination between your home-country physicians and the Bangalore transplant team. Ensure that all your recent medical records, including liver function tests, imaging studies, biopsy results, and viral hepatitis panels, are shared with the Indian hospital in advance. This allows the transplant team to review your case and request any additional tests before your arrival, minimising delays.

In the weeks leading up to the transplant, you may be advised to optimise your nutritional status, as malnutrition is common in end-stage liver disease and can impair wound healing. Alcohol and tobacco must be completely avoided, and you should enrol in a cessation programme if needed.

Certain medications may need to be stopped or adjusted before surgery. Blood-thinning medications are typically discontinued several days in advance. Provide a complete list of all medications, supplements, and herbal remedies to your transplant team. Your vaccinations should be up to date, particularly for hepatitis A and B, influenza, pneumococcal disease, and COVID-19, because post-transplant immunosuppression increases your susceptibility to infection.

## Procedure Details

Your transplant journey begins with a thorough evaluation of both you and your living donor. This includes blood tests, CT and MRI imaging, liver biopsy, tissue typing, and psychosocial assessments. Your donor will undergo detailed anatomical mapping of the liver's blood vessels and bile ducts to determine which portion can be safely donated. Once compatibility is confirmed and the transplant authorisation committee grants approval, your surgery is scheduled.

A living-donor liver transplant involves two simultaneous operations in adjacent operating theatres. The donor hepatectomy removes a portion of your donor's liver, typically the right lobe for an adult recipient or the left lobe for a paediatric recipient. At the same time, your diseased liver is carefully removed while preserving vital blood vessels and bile ducts. The donor graft is then implanted with meticulous vascular and biliary reconstructions to restore blood flow and bile drainage.

The surgery lasts between 8 and 12 hours under general anaesthesia. After the graft is revascularised, the surgical team checks for bleeding and confirms bile production before closing the incision. Your donor is monitored in a separate recovery unit and typically spends 5 to 7 days in hospital before discharge.

Post-operative care spans several weeks in the hospital followed by extended monitoring in Bangalore. Your transplanted liver begins functioning almost immediately, and you will be gradually transitioned from the ICU to a private room. Regular blood tests, ultrasound scans, and liver biopsies if needed will monitor graft function. You will receive detailed counselling on medication adherence, dietary restrictions, and signs of complications before you are cleared to travel home.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the day of surgery, both you and your donor are admitted early in the morning and prepared in separate pre-operative areas. The two surgical teams work simultaneously to minimise the time the new liver is without blood flow.

Your donor's surgery involves a careful incision to expose the liver, dividing the blood vessels and bile ducts supplying the portion to be donated (typically the right lobe), and removing the graft for immediate flushing with preservation solution. Your donor typically spends 5 to 7 days in hospital.

In your operating theatre, your diseased liver is removed while preserving the vena cava and key vascular structures. The donor graft is implanted by connecting the hepatic veins, portal vein, hepatic artery, and bile duct. Once blood flow is restored, the graft is observed for perfusion and bile production. The procedure takes 8 to 12 hours. After surgery, you are transferred to the transplant ICU for 24 to 48 hours, where blood tests and imaging confirm vascular patency. You will be ventilated briefly until stable.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

Your first few days after a liver transplant are spent in the ICU, where you are continuously monitored for liver function, bleeding, infection, and signs of rejection. Blood tests are performed daily to track liver enzymes, clotting factors, and immunosuppressive drug levels. Doppler ultrasound confirms blood flow through the hepatic artery and portal vein. If concerns arise, a liver biopsy may be performed to assess for rejection.

Pain management is a priority, with intravenous and oral analgesics gradually reduced as your healing progresses. Early mobilisation is encouraged, with physiotherapists assisting you to sit up, stand, and walk within the first few days to prevent blood clots and pneumonia.

As you stabilise, you are transferred to a private room. Your immunosuppressive medications are carefully titrated, and you begin learning about your medication schedule, side effects, and the critical importance of adherence. Dietitians guide your transition from intravenous nutrition to oral intake. Wound care, hygiene practices, and infection prevention measures are taught to both you and your attendants.

Throughout your hospital stay, the transplant team watches for complications such as biliary leaks, vascular thrombosis, infections, and acute rejection. Most complications, if detected early, can be managed successfully. You are typically discharged after 2 to 4 weeks, depending on your recovery progress.

Before you are cleared to fly home, your transplant team will assess your fitness to travel, confirming stable liver function, adequate wound healing, and safe immunosuppressive drug levels. Flying too soon after a major abdominal surgery carries risks such as deep vein thrombosis and wound complications, so your team will not sign off on travel until they are confident your body can handle the journey.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

Leading transplant centres in Bangalore report one-year survival rates of 90-95% and graft survival rates of 88-92%, comparable to international benchmarks. Five-year survival rates are typically 75-85%. Your outcomes are influenced by your overall health, the quality of the donor organ, the underlying cause of your liver disease, and your strict adherence to post-transplant medication and follow-up.

For most people, a successful liver transplant dramatically improves quality of life. If you have been debilitated by fatigue, jaundice, ascites, and encephalopathy, you can often return to near-normal activity levels within 3 to 6 months. Your transplanted liver regenerates to near-full size within weeks, and long-term liver function is typically excellent as long as you maintain medication adherence.

However, lifelong immunosuppressive therapy carries ongoing risks. Because your immune system is deliberately suppressed, you will be more susceptible to infections, including opportunistic infections. You also face a higher incidence of certain cancers, particularly skin cancers and lymphomas, and potential metabolic complications such as diabetes and kidney impairment. Regular monitoring and proactive management of these risks are essential.

## Follow-Up Care After Returning Home

After you return to your home country, you must continue close coordination with both your Bangalore transplant team and a local hepatologist or gastroenterologist. In the first few months, blood tests are typically required weekly to monitor your liver function and immunosuppressive drug levels, with frequency gradually decreasing as stability is confirmed. Your Bangalore team remains available for remote consultation.

Your immunosuppressive medications must be taken exactly as prescribed, at the same times each day, without missing doses. Any changes should only be made in consultation with your transplant team. You should also be vigilant about infection prevention, avoiding crowded places and sick contacts, practising meticulous hand hygiene, and staying up to date with recommended vaccinations.

Seek immediate medical attention if you experience fever, jaundice, abdominal pain, severe fatigue, or any signs of infection, as these may indicate rejection or a serious complication. Regular screening for skin cancer, cardiovascular risk, and metabolic complications should be part of your long-term follow-up plan.

## Alternatives to Consider

Before pursuing a liver transplant, explore all other treatment options with your hepatologist. If you have cirrhosis, management of the underlying cause, such as antiviral therapy for hepatitis B or C, alcohol abstinence, or treatment of autoimmune conditions, may stabilise or even improve your liver function sufficiently to delay or avoid transplantation. If you have early-stage hepatocellular carcinoma, you may be a candidate for localised treatments such as radiofrequency ablation or transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE).

If you have portal hypertension complications such as variceal bleeding or refractory ascites, interventions such as endoscopic band ligation or transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) may provide symptomatic relief without the need for a transplant.

A liver transplant is generally reserved for situations where your liver disease has progressed to the point where other treatments can no longer sustain life or acceptable quality of life. The decision should be made jointly by you, your family, your hepatologist, and the transplant team.

## Travel & Visa

You will need a medical visa (e-Medical Visa) to travel to Bangalore for a liver transplant, which you can apply for online through the Indian government's e-Visa portal. The hospital will provide a medical visa invitation letter once your evaluation and scheduling are confirmed. The medical visa is typically valid for 60 days and can be extended if a longer stay is required. Your donor and accompanying attendants should apply for medical attendant visas.

Plan to arrive in Bangalore at least one week before your scheduled surgery date to complete pre-transplant evaluations, donor workup, and committee approvals. It is strongly advisable to travel with one or two attendants who can support you during the hospital stay and recovery period. The hospital's international patient team will arrange airport pickup, accommodation guidance, and local transportation.

Ensure that you carry all medical records, donor relationship documents, and pathology reports in original or attested copies. Documents typically include birth certificates or family registry records proving the donor-recipient relationship, recent imaging studies, biopsy reports, and a referral letter from your home-country hepatologist. All documents should be in English or accompanied by certified translations.
