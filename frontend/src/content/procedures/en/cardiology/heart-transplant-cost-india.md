## Overview

If your doctor has told you that a heart transplant may be your best option, you are probably feeling a mix of hope and worry. That is completely natural. A heart transplant is a major decision, but it is also one that has given thousands of people around the world a second chance at life. Understanding what lies ahead can help you feel more in control and less anxious about the journey.

A heart transplant involves replacing your failing heart with a healthy heart from a donor who has been declared brain-dead. It is recommended when your heart can no longer pump enough blood to meet your body's needs, despite all other treatments having been tried. You may have been living with severe heart failure for some time, and by this stage, medications and other surgeries have done all they can.

The good news is that heart transplantation has excellent outcomes. About 90% of people survive the first year after surgery, and many go on to live active, fulfilling lives for years or even decades. Bangalore's transplant centres are among India's most experienced, with programmes accredited by the National Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisation (NOTTO). For international patients travelling from countries like Bangladesh, the Gulf nations, or across Africa, Bangalore offers shorter waiting times and costs up to 85% lower than in the United States or Europe, making this life-saving procedure far more within reach.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

A heart transplant is considered when your heart failure has reached its most advanced stage and other treatments are no longer enough. You may be a candidate if you have been on the strongest possible medications, possibly with a pacemaker or defibrillator, and possibly after bypass or valve surgery, yet your heart is still failing. The most common conditions that lead to this point include damage from previous heart attacks, a weakened heart muscle (cardiomyopathy) caused by genetics or a viral infection, or restrictive heart disease.

There are several things your transplant team will look at beyond your heart condition. Most programmes accept patients up to 65 years old, though if you are older and otherwise very healthy, you may still be considered. Your lung blood pressure matters too, because the donor heart needs to be able to pump against it. If you have an active infection, untreated cancer, or severe damage to other organs like your kidneys or liver, the team may advise against a transplant until those issues are addressed.

Your emotional readiness and support network matter just as much as your physical health. After a transplant, you will need to take medications every single day for the rest of your life, attend regular clinic visits, and have heart biopsies. If you are travelling from abroad, it is essential that you have a cardiologist or transplant team in your home country who can take over your ongoing care once you return.

## Pre-Operative Preparation

Before you can be placed on the transplant waiting list, you will go through a thorough evaluation that usually takes about five to seven days. Think of this as your team making sure your body is ready for what lies ahead. You will have a detailed echocardiogram, heart catheterisation to measure pressures in your heart and lungs, and possibly a cardiac MRI or CT scan. Your lung, liver, and kidney function will be checked, and you will be screened for infections including hepatitis, HIV, and tuberculosis.

Your blood type and tissue type will be recorded so the team can find the best possible donor match for you. You will also have a dental check-up, because any hidden infection could cause problems after surgery. A psychiatrist or counsellor will assess how you are coping, and a dietitian will evaluate your nutrition. Any vaccinations you need will be given now, because once you start on immunosuppressive medications, live vaccines will no longer be safe for you.

Once the evaluation is complete and you are accepted onto the waiting list, you will need to stay in or near Bangalore so you can come in quickly when a donor heart becomes available. The wait can range from a few days to several months, depending on your blood type, body size, and how urgent your case is. During this time, your transplant team will stay in close contact with you and adjust your medications to keep you as strong as possible for surgery.

## Procedure Details

Your heart transplant will be performed under general anaesthesia, so you will be completely asleep and will not feel anything. The surgery itself usually takes about four to six hours. The timing is carefully coordinated with the organ retrieval team so that your donor heart arrives at the operating theatre at just the right moment, keeping the time the heart is without blood flow as short as possible.

While the donor heart is being transported, you will be prepared for surgery. Your surgeon will carefully remove your diseased heart, leaving behind the back walls of your upper heart chambers and the openings of the major veins as connection points for your new heart. The donor heart is then connected step by step, first the left side, then the right side, followed by the pulmonary artery and the aorta.

Once all the connections are complete, the surgeon will remove the air from the heart and release the clamp on your aorta. Warm blood will flow into your new heart, and it will usually begin to beat on its own within minutes. You will then be gradually taken off the heart-lung machine as your new heart takes over. Surgical drains and temporary pacing wires will be placed, your chest will be closed, and you will be transferred to the transplant intensive care unit.

## What Happens During the Procedure

After your surgery, you will wake up in the transplant ICU, where you will be closely monitored around the clock. Your team will start your immunosuppressive medications immediately to help your body accept the new heart. You can expect to stay in the ICU for about five to ten days, and your total hospital stay will be around two to three weeks.

Within the first week, your team will perform a heart biopsy to check that your body is not rejecting the new heart. This involves taking a tiny sample of heart tissue through a small tube inserted into a vein in your neck. Further biopsies will be scheduled at regular intervals so your team can catch any signs of rejection early.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

The first few days after your transplant are the most critical, and you will be in the transplant ICU with constant monitoring. You will have frequent blood tests to check your medication levels, organ function, and any signs of infection. Your immunosuppressive medications will be carefully adjusted to protect your new heart while keeping your risk of infection as low as possible. It is normal to feel tired and sore during this time, but your nurses will help manage any pain.

As you stabilise, you will move from the ICU to a transplant ward. Your rehabilitation will begin gently, starting with sitting up and walking short distances, and gradually building up. A big part of this phase is education. Your team will teach you about your medications, how to recognise signs of infection, how to care for your wound, and why it is so important never to miss a dose. A dietitian will guide you on food safety, because your weakened immune system means you will need to avoid certain foods.

After your hospital stay of two to three weeks, you will need to remain in Bangalore for another two to four weeks. During this time, you will attend frequent outpatient appointments for biopsies, blood tests, and medication adjustments. This extended stay is essential because the risk of rejection is highest in the first three months. You will only be cleared to travel home once your transplant team is confident that your medications are stable and there are no signs of rejection.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

Heart transplantation offers genuinely life-changing results for most people. According to international registry data, about 90% of recipients survive the first year, 75 to 80% survive five years, and 60 to 70% are still doing well at ten years. Most people experience a dramatic improvement in their quality of life, returning to work, exercise, and social activities that had become impossible before the transplant.

It is important to be honest about the challenges too. The leading causes of later complications include cardiac allograft vasculopathy, a form of chronic rejection that causes narrowing of the arteries in your new heart, opportunistic infections, and certain cancers related to long-term immunosuppression. Your transplant team will monitor you for all of these with regular check-ups and tests.

Your long-term success depends heavily on taking your medications exactly as prescribed and attending all your follow-up appointments. Once your transplant team clears you, moderate exercise is encouraged and beneficial. If you are hoping to have children, pregnancy is possible after a transplant but requires careful planning and medication adjustments, so always discuss this with your transplant team first.

## Follow-Up Care After Returning Home

When you return to your home country, you will transition to a local cardiologist or transplant team for your ongoing care. Your Bangalore team will provide a detailed medical summary, surgical report, your immunosuppression regimen, and all your biopsy results to your home team.

Taking your immunosuppressive medications every day, without fail, is the single most important thing you will do for your new heart. Your tacrolimus blood levels will need to be checked regularly, and your doses adjusted accordingly. You will need to be vigilant about infections, reporting any fever, rash, or new symptom to your doctor immediately. Annual screening for skin cancer and other malignancies is recommended. Khan Meditour remains available to help with communication between you, your home team, and your Bangalore transplant centre whenever clinical questions arise.

## Alternatives to Consider

If a heart transplant is not suitable for you, or if the waiting time is too long, there are other options. A left ventricular assist device, or LVAD, is an implantable pump that takes over the work of your failing left ventricle. It can be used as a bridge while you wait for a transplant, or as a long-term solution if transplant is not an option. Bangalore's cardiac centres offer LVAD implantation.

Sometimes, optimising your heart failure medications with the latest drugs can significantly improve your symptoms and may even delay or eliminate the need for a transplant. A biventricular pacemaker can also help if you have specific electrical problems in your heart. If your heart failure is caused by something that can be fixed, such as severe valve disease or blocked arteries, corrective surgery may be a better option. Your transplant team will carefully evaluate all alternatives before recommending that you go on the waiting list.

You may be wondering why a heart transplant in India costs so much less than in the United States or Europe. The lower cost does not mean lower quality. India's lower cost of living, lower staff salaries, and high volume of procedures allow hospitals to offer the same world-class care at a fraction of the price. The surgeons are highly trained, many with international experience, and the facilities are accredited and equipped with the latest technology. The package typically includes the transplant surgery, donor organ procurement, your hospital stay, and initial post-operative care. Long-term immunosuppressive medications and follow-up biopsies may incur additional costs over time, so it is wise to budget for those.

You will travel to Bangalore on a Medical Visa, which is available as an e-Medical Visa for citizens of over 160 countries and is usually granted within 48 to 72 hours. The visa is valid for up to one year with triple entry, which accommodates your transplant listing period, the surgery itself, and any follow-up visits. Khan Meditour will help you with the visa invitation letter from the transplant centre, scheduling your evaluation, and arranging all your ground logistics.

When you arrive at Kempegowda International Airport, a private driver will meet you and take you to the hospital. The transplant evaluation takes about five to seven days, and after you are listed, you will need to stay in or near Bangalore so you can come in quickly when a donor heart is found.

Here are some practical things to keep in mind:

- **Plan for a 4 to 8 week stay** in total, covering the evaluation, waiting period, surgery, and initial recovery
- **Bring a family member or close friend** with you. You will need emotional support and practical help, especially during the waiting period and after surgery
- **Pack comfortable, loose clothing** that is easy to put on after surgery, as your chest will be sore
- **Bring all your medical records organised in a folder**, including previous echo reports, angiograms, medication lists, and doctor referrals
- **Bring enough of your current medications** to last the first few weeks, along with prescriptions
- **Family accommodation** can be arranged near the hospital, and Khan Meditour will help with this

Most surgeons recommend waiting at least 7-14 days before taking a long-haul flight, to reduce the risk of deep vein thrombosis and other travel-related complications. Khan Meditour can arrange a fitness-to-fly certificate from your medical team and advise you on in-flight precautions such as compression stockings, hydration, and periodic movement during the journey.
