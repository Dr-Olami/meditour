## Overview

Learning that you or your child has a hole in the heart can be frightening. You may have been told it is called an atrial septal defect (ASD) or ventricular septal defect (VSD), and you are probably wondering what this means for daily life, for the future, and for the surgery that may lie ahead. Take a breath — these are among the most common congenital heart conditions, and the good news is that closure surgery has an excellent success rate, often exceeding 98%. After repair, most people go on to live completely normal lives.

ASD and VSD are holes in the wall (septum) that separates your heart's chambers. These defects allow oxygen-rich blood to mix with oxygen-poor blood, making your heart and lungs work harder than they should. Over time, significant defects can lead to right heart enlargement, pulmonary hypertension, arrhythmias and eventually heart failure if left untreated. While some small defects may close on their own during childhood, larger or persistent defects almost always need surgical or device-based intervention.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

ASD/VSD closure surgery is recommended if you or your child has been diagnosed with a significant septal defect that is causing or is likely to cause harm to the heart and lungs. The decision to proceed is based on the size and location of the defect, the degree of blood shunting across it, and whether you are experiencing symptoms. If your pulmonary-to-systemic blood flow ratio (Qp:Qs) is greater than 1.5:1, closure is generally recommended even if you have no symptoms, because the cumulative effect of long-term shunting leads to progressive right heart dilation.

You may be experiencing breathlessness on exertion, fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance or recurrent respiratory infections. In more advanced cases, you may have developed atrial arrhythmias, signs of right heart failure such as leg swelling, or paradoxical embolism resulting in stroke. Many adults with previously undiagnosed ASDs present in their fourth or fifth decade with these symptoms — and closure can dramatically improve your quality of life.

Not every septal defect requires open-heart surgery. Secundum ASDs with adequate tissue rims may be suitable for percutaneous device closure in the catheterisation laboratory, which avoids sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass. However, large defects, multiple defects, primum ASDs, sinus venosus defects and most VSDs require surgical closure. You must not have developed Eisenmenger syndrome, as closure is contraindicated once pulmonary vascular disease becomes irreversible.

## Pre-Operative Preparation

Before your surgery is scheduled, you will undergo a comprehensive cardiac evaluation. This includes a detailed transthoracic echocardiogram to assess defect size, location and any associated cardiac anomalies. In some cases, a transoesophageal echocardiogram or cardiac MRI may be required for more precise visualisation.

If you are an adult, a coronary angiogram is typically performed to rule out coexisting coronary artery disease. Cardiac catheterisation may also be necessary to measure your pulmonary artery pressures and calculate pulmonary vascular resistance — this is critical for determining whether closure is safe. Blood tests, a chest X-ray and an electrocardiogram complete the workup. If you are on blood-thinning medications, you will be advised when to stop them, and smoking cessation is strongly recommended.

## Procedure Details

ASD/VSD closure is performed under general anaesthesia with cardiopulmonary bypass (a heart-lung machine) and typically takes 2–3 hours. The surgeon opens your chest through a median sternotomy, and your heart is arrested with cold cardioplegia solution. The right atrium is opened to access the ASD, or the right atrium or pulmonary artery to access the VSD, depending on its location and size. The heart-lung machine takes over circulating and oxygenating your blood while your heart is stopped.

For an ASD, the defect is closed either by direct suture (for small defects with adequate tissue edges) or by sewing in a patch — your own pericardial tissue or a synthetic patch (Dacron or Gore-Tex) — that covers the hole completely. For a VSD, the surgeon visualises the defect through the tricuspid valve and closes it with a patch using continuous or interrupted pledgeted sutures. Special care is taken to avoid injury to the conduction tissue near the rim of many VSDs, which could cause heart block if damaged.

Intraoperative transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is used throughout to confirm complete closure and assess valve function. After the procedure, your heart is restarted, bypass is discontinued, and your chest is closed with wires. You will spend 24–48 hours in the cardiac ICU with continuous monitoring, and a post-operative echocardiogram will confirm complete defect closure.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the day of surgery, you will be taken to the operating theatre and general anaesthesia will be administered. Monitoring lines will be placed, including an arterial line for continuous blood pressure measurement and a central venous line for medication administration. The TEE probe will be inserted to provide real-time imaging of your heart throughout the operation.

Once your heart and major vessels are exposed, you will be heparinised and connected to the cardiopulmonary bypass machine. Your heart will be arrested with cold cardioplegia solution, providing a still and bloodless field for the surgeon to work. The septal defect will be identified and closed using the appropriate technique — direct suture or patch closure — depending on the size and anatomy. The surgeon will inspect the repair carefully, ensuring there are no residual gaps and that nearby valves are not compromised.

After the closure is complete, your heart will be de-aired, the aortic clamp released, and your heart allowed to resume beating. You will be gradually weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass, and the function of the repair confirmed with TEE. Pacing wires and chest drains will be placed, the sternum reapproximated with stainless steel wires, and the soft tissues and skin closed in layers. The entire procedure typically takes 2–3 hours.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

After surgery, you will be transferred to the cardiac intensive care unit for close monitoring. During the first 24–48 hours, the medical team will track your heart rhythm, blood pressure, oxygen saturation and chest tube output. Pain is managed with intravenous medications that are gradually transitioned to oral analgesics. Most patients are extubated within a few hours and can begin sitting up and taking sips of water on the first post-operative day.

**Will it hurt?** You will have some pain around your chest incision, particularly when coughing or deep breathing. This is well managed with pain medication and gradually subsides over the first week. Early mobilisation is a key part of your recovery — you will be encouraged to sit in a chair, stand and walk short distances with assistance by the second or third day. Chest tubes are typically removed within 48–72 hours. A repeat echocardiogram is performed before discharge to confirm the defect is fully closed.

Most patients are discharged between 4 and 7 days after surgery. During your remaining 1–2 weeks in Bangalore, you will attend follow-up appointments for wound review and a final echocardiogram before being cleared to fly home. You should avoid heavy lifting, strenuous exercise and driving for approximately six weeks to allow your sternum to heal.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

Surgical closure of ASDs and VSDs is associated with excellent long-term outcomes. Complete closure is achieved in over 98% of cases, and the vast majority of patients experience significant improvement in symptoms and exercise capacity. Your right heart dilation will regress over months to years following closure, and the risk of progressive pulmonary hypertension is halted — provided the operation is performed before irreversible vascular changes have occurred.

Long-term survival after ASD closure in adults approaches that of the general population, particularly when closure is performed before the age of 25. If you are closed later in life, you still benefit substantially, though you may have some residual right heart dysfunction or persistent atrial arrhythmias. Lifelong cardiology surveillance is recommended, with regular follow-up echocardiograms at one year post-operatively and periodically thereafter.

## Alternatives to Consider

For selected patients with secundum ASDs, percutaneous device closure using an Amplatzer septal occluder is a well-established alternative to surgery. This catheter-based approach avoids a sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass, resulting in a shorter hospital stay and faster recovery. However, it is only suitable for defects with adequate surrounding tissue rims and is not applicable to primum, sinus venosus or coronary sinus defects, nor to most VSDs.

For very small ASDs or VSDs with minimal shunting and no evidence of cardiac enlargement, a conservative approach with regular echocardiographic surveillance may be appropriate. Some small VSDs in children close spontaneously as the child grows. However, you should be aware that untreated significant defects carry a cumulative risk of complications, and the window for safe closure may close if pulmonary hypertension progresses to an irreversible stage. Your cardiac team in Bangalore will discuss all available options and recommend the most appropriate strategy for your specific anatomy.
