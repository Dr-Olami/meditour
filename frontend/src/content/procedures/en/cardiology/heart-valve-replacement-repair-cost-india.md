## Overview

Heart valve surgery encompasses procedures to repair or replace diseased heart valves that are narrowed (stenosis) or leaking (regurgitation). The four heart valves—mitral, aortic, tricuspid, and pulmonary—ensure one-way blood flow through the heart's chambers. When a valve malfunctions, the heart must work harder, leading to breathlessness, fatigue, chest pain, and eventually heart failure if left untreated.

Valve repair preserves the patient's native tissue and is preferred when the valve anatomy allows, particularly for mitral valve regurgitation. Valve replacement uses either mechanical valves (durable, lifelong, but requiring anticoagulation) or biological tissue valves (no long-term blood thinners, but limited lifespan). ## Procedure Details

Valve surgery is performed under general anaesthesia with cardiopulmonary bypass (heart-lung machine) and typically takes 2-4 hours. The surgeon opens the chest through a median sternotomy (or a minimally invasive approach in selected cases), and the heart is arrested with cold cardioplegia solution. The diseased valve is exposed and assessed for repairability.

For valve repair, techniques include leaflet resection, chordal reconstruction, annuloplasty ring placement, and commissurotomy. For valve replacement, the diseased valve is excised and the annulus is measured. The prosthetic valve—mechanical or biological—is then sutured into position using a series of pledgeted sutures. Intraoperative transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is used to confirm proper valve function before weaning from bypass.

After the procedure, the heart is restarted, the bypass is discontinued, and the chest is closed with wires. Patients spend 24-48 hours in the cardiac ICU, where cardiac monitoring, anticoagulation initiation (for mechanical valves), and early mobilisation begin. Transfer to the ward follows, with discharge typically at 5-8 days. Post-discharge care includes wound monitoring, anticoagulation management, and a repeat echocardiogram before flying home.
