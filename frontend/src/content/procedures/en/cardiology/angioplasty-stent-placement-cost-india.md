## Overview

If you have just been told that one or more of your coronary arteries is narrowed or blocked, you are probably feeling a mix of fear, confusion, and urgency. Hearing words like "blocked artery" and "stent" for the first time is overwhelming. The good news is that coronary angioplasty—also called percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI)—is one of the most commonly performed and well-refined heart procedures in the world, and it does not involve open-heart surgery.

During angioplasty, your cardiologist threads a thin, flexible tube called a catheter through a small needle puncture in your wrist or groin and guides it to the blocked coronary artery. A tiny balloon at the tip is inflated to compress the plaque against the artery wall, and a stent—a small wire mesh tube—is placed to hold the vessel open. Because the procedure is done through a needle puncture rather than a chest incision, you avoid the long recovery and intense pain associated with open-heart surgery. You will be under local anaesthesia with mild sedation, and most people go home within one to three days.

Angioplasty is used for two main situations. If you are having an acute heart attack, emergency angioplasty can open the blocked artery quickly and limit damage to your heart muscle. If you have stable coronary artery disease with chest pain or shortness of breath that is not adequately controlled by medication alone, elective angioplasty can relieve your symptoms. Bangalore's interventional cardiologists use the latest-generation drug-eluting stents and advanced imaging tools such as intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) and optical coherence tomography (OCT) to optimise stent placement.

For international patients, Bangalore offers a compelling combination: round-the-clock catheterisation laboratories, highly experienced interventional teams, and costs that are up to 85% lower than in Western countries. Dedicated international patient teams coordinate every step of your journey.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

Angioplasty with stent placement may be right for you if you have significant coronary artery disease where the blockages can be reached with a catheter. The most common scenario is single or double-vessel disease with discrete, accessible blockages in arteries of adequate size (typically 2.5mm or greater). If you have stable angina that is not well controlled by medications alone, or if stress testing shows evidence of reduced blood flow to your heart muscle, you may be an excellent candidate for elective PCI.

If you are experiencing acute coronary syndrome—including unstable angina or an acute heart attack—angioplasty is the preferred emergency treatment. Primary PCI, performed as quickly as possible after your symptoms begin, opens the blocked artery and restores blood flow, which can minimise damage to your heart muscle. Bangalore's cardiac centres maintain 24/7 catheterisation laboratory availability for these emergencies.

If you have had a stent before and the artery has narrowed again (in-stent restenosis), or if you have a chronic total occlusion, you may still be a candidate, though these cases are more complex. Your interventional cardiologist will assess your coronary angiogram, overall cardiac function, and general health to determine whether PCI is the best approach for you, or whether bypass surgery would offer better long-term outcomes.

## Pre-Operative Preparation

Before you travel to Bangalore, share all your relevant medical records with your treating cardiologist for advance review. This includes any recent coronary angiograms (on CD or USB), echocardiography reports, stress test results, blood work, and a complete list of every medication and supplement you take. If you take blood-thinning medications such as warfarin, you may need to stop them a few days before the procedure, while aspirin is usually continued. Be sure to disclose everything you take, including over-the-counter supplements.

Once you arrive in Bangalore, you will undergo a comprehensive pre-procedure assessment. This includes a detailed review of your coronary angiogram, blood tests (kidney function, coagulation profile, blood group, lipid panel), an electrocardiogram, and an echocardiogram. If you have pre-existing kidney disease, your team may give you intravenous fluids before and after the procedure to protect your kidneys from the contrast dye. Your cardiologist will explain the planned procedure, the expected number of stents, and the potential risks, and will ask for your informed consent. You will be asked to fast for 6 to 8 hours before the procedure.

## Procedure Details

Your angioplasty takes place in a specialised room called a cardiac catheterisation laboratory. You will receive local anaesthesia at the access site—most commonly the radial artery in your wrist, which lets you sit up and walk sooner and carries a lower risk of bleeding complications than the groin approach. You will also be given mild sedation through an IV, so you will feel relaxed and possibly drowsy, but you will not be under general anaesthesia. The procedure itself typically takes 30 to 90 minutes.

After numbing your wrist, your cardiologist inserts a small sheath and advances a guiding catheter to the origin of your coronary arteries. Contrast dye is injected so the blockages can be seen on live X-ray imaging (fluoroscopy). A guidewire is then carefully passed across the blockage, and a balloon catheter is threaded over the wire to the narrowing. The balloon is inflated to high pressure, compressing the plaque and widening the artery. A drug-eluting stent mounted on a balloon is positioned at the lesion and deployed by inflating the balloon. The stent stays in your artery permanently, acting as a scaffold to keep it open.

Once your cardiologist confirms the result with repeat angiography or imaging, the catheters are removed and the access site is sealed with a closure device or a compression band. You will be monitored in the cardiac care unit for 12 to 24 hours. During this time, dual antiplatelet therapy is started to prevent blood clots inside the new stent. Most people are discharged within one to three days with clear medication instructions and a follow-up plan.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the day of your procedure, you will be taken to the cardiac catheterisation laboratory. The nursing team will start an intravenous line, give you mild sedation, and attach cardiac monitoring leads to your chest. The access site—usually your right wrist—is cleaned and numbed with local anaesthetic. You will remain awake throughout the procedure and able to communicate with your cardiologist, though you may feel drowsy from the sedation.

Your cardiologist inserts a small sheath into the radial artery and advances a guiding catheter to the origin of your coronary arteries. Contrast dye is injected so the blockages can be seen on fluoroscopy. Once the target lesion is identified, a guidewire is passed across the blockage and a balloon catheter is advanced over the wire and inflated to compress the plaque and widen the artery. You may feel brief chest discomfort during balloon inflation—this is normal and passes quickly.

A drug-eluting stent is then positioned at the lesion and deployed by balloon inflation. The stent expands and presses against the artery wall, where it will remain permanently. Your cardiologist confirms adequate stent placement with repeat angiography and may use intravascular imaging (IVUS or OCT) for additional precision. Once the result is satisfactory, the catheters are removed and the access site is sealed. The entire procedure typically takes 30 to 90 minutes.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

After the procedure, you will be transferred to the cardiac care unit for close monitoring. The nursing staff will watch your radial access site for any bleeding, and the compression band will be gradually loosened over several hours. Dual antiplatelet therapy—aspirin plus clopidogrel or ticagrelor—is started immediately to prevent clots from forming inside your new stent. Most people can sit up and eat within a few hours and are encouraged to drink plenty of fluids to help flush the contrast dye from their system.

You will likely be discharged within one to three days, depending on the complexity of your procedure and whether it was elective or emergency. Before you go home, your cardiologist will review the procedure results, confirm stent placement with an echocardiogram, and give you detailed medication instructions. You will be counselled on the critical importance of taking your dual antiplatelet therapy exactly as prescribed for 6 to 12 months—stopping these medications prematurely dramatically increases your risk of a dangerous blood clot inside the stent. Activity restrictions are minimal: most people can resume normal daily activities within a week.

During your remaining time in Bangalore, a follow-up review will be scheduled to check your access site, assess your cardiac function, and provide medication counselling. A fitness-to-fly certificate will be issued once your cardiologist confirms you are safe to travel. Always carry your stent information card when you travel.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

Angioplasty with drug-eluting stents has excellent procedural success rates exceeding 95% in experienced centres, with restenosis rates below 5% at one year. Bangalore's interventional cardiology teams achieve outcomes consistent with international benchmarks, thanks to high procedural volumes and access to the latest-generation stent technology. If you are treated for an acute heart attack, timely primary PCI significantly reduces mortality compared to clot-busting medication alone and is the gold-standard treatment.

Your long-term success, however, depends on more than the procedure itself. The underlying disease process that caused your blockage continues after stenting, and new blockages can develop in other coronary arteries. Taking your statins, antiplatelet therapy, blood pressure medication, and diabetes medication as prescribed, along with smoking cessation and a heart-healthy lifestyle, is essential to prevent disease progression. Regular follow-up with a cardiologist, annual lipid and blood pressure checks, and stress testing if your symptoms return are all recommended.

## Follow-Up Care After Returning Home

Before you leave Bangalore, you will receive a comprehensive discharge summary detailing your procedure, the stent type and specifications, the medications prescribed, and follow-up recommendations. Share this document with your cardiologist at home, who will take over your ongoing care. A follow-up cardiology appointment is typically scheduled at 4 to 6 weeks, with regular cardiac check-ups at least annually thereafter. If you are on dual antiplatelet therapy, it is critical that you continue both medications exactly as prescribed.

Seek immediate medical attention if you experience new or worsening chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations, or signs of bleeding. Any return of angina-type symptoms should be evaluated promptly, as it may indicate a clot inside your stent or disease progression. If you need surgery or dental procedures in the future, tell your treating physician about your stent and your antiplatelet therapy. ## Alternatives to Consider

If you have complex, multi-vessel coronary artery disease—especially if you have diabetes, left main disease, or diffuse lesions—coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) may offer better long-term outcomes than angioplasty. Your cardiology team will weigh the risks and benefits of PCI versus CABG based on your coronary anatomy, overall health, and individual risk profile.

If you have stable coronary artery disease with mild to moderate symptoms, optimal medical therapy (OMT) alone may be a reasonable first approach. Modern medications—including high-intensity statins, antiplatelet agents, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, and nitrates—can effectively control symptoms and reduce cardiac events in selected people. Angioplasty can then be reserved for those whose symptoms remain uncontrolled by medication.
