## Overview

If you have been experiencing dizzy spells, unexplained fatigue, or episodes of fainting, and your doctor has told you that your heart rate is too slow, you may be feeling frightened and confused. You are probably wondering what is wrong with your heart's electrical system and whether a pacemaker will really help. Take a breath — a pacemaker is one of the most reliable and life-changing devices in modern medicine. It is small, it is safe, and it can give you back the energy and stability that slow heart rhythms have taken away.

A pacemaker is a small electronic device implanted under your skin below your collarbone. It consists of a pulse generator (a battery and electronic circuitry) and one or more leads (thin wires) that deliver electrical impulses to your heart muscle, ensuring it beats at a normal rate and rhythm. Pacemakers are indicated when your natural pacemaker (the SA node) or electrical conduction system (the AV node) is diseased or damaged.

Bangalore's cardiac centres implant pacemakers from leading global manufacturers including Medtronic, Boston Scientific, Abbott and Biotronik, with both single-chamber and dual-chamber systems available. The procedure is minimally invasive, performed under local anaesthesia, and typically requires only a short hospital stay — making it well suited for medical travel with minimal disruption to your daily life. You will save up to 80% compared to Western pricing.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

A pacemaker may be recommended if you have documented bradyarrhythmias — abnormally slow heart rates — that cause symptoms such as dizziness, light-headedness, fatigue, exercise intolerance or fainting. The most common indications include high-grade atrioventricular block, complete heart block and sick sinus syndrome with symptomatic pauses.

If you have atrial fibrillation accompanied by a slow ventricular response, you may need pacing support to maintain an adequate heart rate. If you have experienced syncope of suspected cardiac origin with conduction abnormalities confirmed on ECG or Holter monitoring, you may also be a candidate. In some cases, pacemakers are implanted after cardiac surgery or catheter ablation when the conduction system has been damaged.

You should have a clear clinical indication documented by a cardiologist. If your bradycardia is caused by medications or reversible conditions, you may not need a permanent device. Your Bangalore electrophysiologist will review your records, confirm the indication and recommend the most appropriate device type — single-chamber, dual-chamber or biventricular — based on your conduction disease and overall cardiac function.

## Pre-Operative Preparation

Preparation begins with sharing your medical records — including recent ECGs, Holter monitor reports and echocardiogram — with the Bangalore electrophysiology team for review. Once you arrive, a comprehensive assessment is conducted over 1–2 days. This includes a detailed clinical evaluation, repeat ECG, blood tests and echocardiography to assess your underlying cardiac structure and function.

Your electrophysiologist will discuss the type of pacemaker recommended and explain the differences between single-chamber and dual-chamber systems. MRI-conditional devices are recommended if you may need future MRI scans. Informed consent will be obtained after a thorough discussion of benefits, risks and alternatives.

If you are on blood thinners, you will be advised on timing of cessation. Fasting for 6–8 hours before the procedure is required. The hospital's international patient desk coordinates admission, pre-operative preparation and scheduling.

## Procedure Details

Your pacemaker implantation is performed in a cardiac catheterisation laboratory under local anaesthesia with mild sedation, typically taking 45–90 minutes. The surgeon makes a small incision (about 5cm) below your collarbone and creates a subcutaneous pocket for the pulse generator. A needle is inserted into the subclavian or cephalic vein, and the pacing lead(s) are advanced through a sheath into your heart under fluoroscopic guidance.

For a dual-chamber pacemaker, one lead is positioned in the right atrial appendage and a second lead in the right ventricular apex. The leads are tested for sensing and pacing thresholds, impedance and stability before being secured to the underlying tissue. The pulse generator is then connected to the leads and placed in the subcutaneous pocket, and the incision is closed with absorbable sutures.

After the procedure, you will be monitored in the cardiac care unit for 12–24 hours. A chest X-ray is performed to confirm lead position and exclude pneumothorax, and your pacemaker is interrogated to verify proper function and programming. Most patients are discharged within 1–3 days with instructions on arm movement restrictions (avoid raising the arm on the implant side above shoulder height for 4–6 weeks), wound care and a follow-up pacemaker check schedule.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the day of your procedure, you will be taken to the cardiac catheterisation laboratory. Local anaesthesia will be administered at the implant site below your collarbone, along with mild intravenous sedation for comfort. The skin will be cleaned and draped sterilely, and a small incision made to create a subcutaneous pocket for the pulse generator.

**Will it hurt?** You will feel some pressure and tugging during the procedure, but it should not be painful thanks to the local anaesthesia. You will remain conscious throughout and able to communicate with the team.

Under fluoroscopic guidance, a needle will be inserted into the subclavian or cephalic vein. A sheath will be placed and the pacing lead(s) advanced through the venous system into your heart. For a dual-chamber device, one lead is positioned in the right atrium and a second in the right ventricle. Each lead is tested for pacing threshold, sensing function and impedance to ensure reliable performance.

Once the leads are secured, the pulse generator will be connected and placed in the subcutaneous pocket. The incision will be closed with absorbable sutures and a sterile dressing applied. Your pacemaker will be interrogated via a programming device to confirm capture, sensing and baseline settings. The entire procedure typically takes 45–90 minutes.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

After the procedure, you will be transferred to the cardiac care unit for 12–24 hours of monitoring. Your heart rhythm will be continuously displayed on telemetry, and the pacemaker pocket observed for bleeding or haematoma formation. A chest X-ray will be performed to confirm lead position and exclude pneumothorax — a known risk of subclavian vein access.

Your pacemaker will be interrogated the following morning to verify pacing and sensing function, and programming optimised for your individual needs. Pain at the implant site is usually mild and managed with simple analgesics. You will be encouraged to mobilise gently, but you must avoid raising the arm on the implant side above shoulder height for 4–6 weeks to prevent lead dislodgement.

Discharge typically occurs within 1–3 days. Before leaving the hospital, you will receive a pacemaker identification card with device details, a wound care guide and a follow-up schedule. During your remaining days in Bangalore, an outpatient device check will be performed to confirm stable function before a fitness-to-fly certificate is issued.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

Pacemaker implantation is one of the most successful cardiac interventions, with symptom resolution in over 95% of patients with documented bradyarrhythmias. Your syncope episodes should be effectively eliminated, exercise tolerance improved and quality of life significantly better within weeks of implantation.

Modern pacemakers are highly reliable, with device-related complications occurring in fewer than 2% of implants at accredited centres. Battery life typically ranges from 6–10 years depending on pacing dependency and programming. When the battery approaches end of life, a straightforward generator change under local anaesthesia replaces the device while retaining the existing leads.

Lifelong surveillance is essential. If you have a dual-chamber or MRI-conditional device, you may benefit from remote monitoring, which transmits device data to the clinic without requiring an in-person visit. Regular checks — initially every 3–6 months, then annually — ensure battery status, lead integrity and pacing thresholds remain optimal.

## Follow-Up Care After Returning Home

After returning home, you should schedule a pacemaker check with your local cardiologist or device clinic within 2–4 weeks. This confirms the device is functioning correctly and allows programming adjustments based on your activity patterns. The discharge summary and device information from Bangalore should be shared with your home team.

Remote monitoring, if available with your device, enables automatic transmission of pacing data to your clinic. Annual in-person checks are recommended to assess battery status and lead integrity. Any swelling, redness or pain at the implant site should be evaluated promptly, as these may indicate pocket infection. ## Alternatives to Consider

If your bradycardia is caused by medications such as beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers, adjusting or discontinuing the offending drug may resolve the problem without a pacemaker. Reversible causes such as electrolyte disturbances, hypothyroidism or transient ischaemia should be excluded before committing to a permanent device.

If you have heart failure and conduction disease, cardiac resynchronisation therapy (CRT) with a biventricular pacemaker may be more appropriate than a conventional device. If you are at risk of life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias, an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) provides both pacing and shock therapy. Your Bangalore electrophysiologist will evaluate your specific condition and recommend the most suitable device.
