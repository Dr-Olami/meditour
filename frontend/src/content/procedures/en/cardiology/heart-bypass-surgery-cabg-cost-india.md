## Overview

Being told you need heart bypass surgery is scary, and you probably have many questions. That's completely understandable — this is one of the most common major heart operations in the world, but when it's happening to you, it feels anything but routine. Let us walk you through what this surgery involves and why Bangalore has become a trusted destination for patients who need world-class cardiac care at a fraction of the cost.

Heart bypass surgery, also called Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting or CABG (pronounced "cabbage"), improves blood flow to your heart. Over time, the arteries that supply your heart can become narrowed or blocked by fatty deposits called plaque. When that happens, your heart doesn't get enough oxygen, causing chest pain (angina), shortness of breath, and in severe cases, heart attacks. During bypass surgery, your surgeon takes healthy blood vessels from another part of your body — usually from inside your chest wall, your leg, or your forearm — and uses them to create new pathways around the blocked arteries, restoring oxygen-rich blood flow.

For many people with multiple blocked arteries, diabetes, or left main artery blockages, research consistently shows that bypass surgery gives better long-term results than stents. Your surgeon in Bangalore will review your angiogram and help you understand which option is truly best for your situation.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

You may be reading this because your cardiologist has told you that your coronary arteries are severely blocked and medications or stents aren't enough. That's a frightening moment, but it helps to understand why bypass surgery might be the best option for you.

CABG is most commonly recommended when you have **multi-vessel disease** — meaning two or more of your heart's major arteries have severe blockages. If you have a blockage in your **left main coronary artery**, bypass surgery is usually the safest and most effective treatment, because this type of blockage carries a high risk of a catastrophic event and often can't be safely stented.

If you have **diabetes**, research shows that diabetic patients with multi-vessel disease do significantly better with bypass surgery than with stents. If you've previously had angioplasty or stenting and the blockages have come back, or if you have diffuse disease spread throughout the artery, bypass surgery is often the better path forward.

CABG may also be right for you if your heart's pumping function is reduced but the heart muscle is still viable. Restoring blood flow can improve how well your heart works over time.

## Pre-Operative Preparation

Before you travel to Bangalore, gather all your medical records — your recent coronary angiogram (on a CD or film), echocardiography reports, blood test results, and a complete list of every medication you take. Share these with your surgeon in advance so they can review your case and plan the operation before you arrive.

Your doctor will likely ask you to stop certain blood-thinning medications — like clopidogrel or ticagrelor — 5 to 7 days before surgery to reduce bleeding risk. Aspirin is usually continued, but don't make any changes without checking first. Be completely honest about everything you take, including herbal supplements, because some can interact with anaesthesia or increase bleeding.

Once you arrive in Bangalore, you'll undergo a comprehensive pre-operative assessment including angiogram review, echocardiogram, blood tests, a chest X-ray, and possibly lung function tests. You'll meet with the anaesthesia team, who will assess your fitness for surgery. You'll be asked to fast for 6 to 8 hours before the procedure.

## Procedure Details

It's natural to feel anxious about what will happen during the surgery. The operation is done under general anaesthesia, meaning you'll be completely asleep and won't feel anything. It typically takes 3 to 5 hours, depending on how many bypasses you need.

There are two main ways your surgeon can perform the operation:

**On-pump surgery** is the traditional approach. Your heart is temporarily stopped, and a heart-lung machine takes over pumping and oxygenating your blood, giving your surgeon a still, quiet field for very precise connections. Once the bypasses are complete, your heart is restarted.

**Off-pump (beating heart) surgery** is performed while your heart continues to beat. Specialised stabilising devices hold just the small area being worked on. This avoids the heart-lung machine, which some patients tolerate better — particularly older patients or those with kidney or lung problems.

To create the bypasses, your surgeon will harvest healthy blood vessels from your body. The most commonly used is the left internal mammary artery (LIMA), which runs inside your chest wall — it has excellent long-term durability, staying open in over 90% of patients even 10 years later. Your surgeon may also use the saphenous vein from your leg, and sometimes the radial artery from your forearm. After the grafts are in place, your sternum (breastbone) is closed with stainless steel wires, and you'll be taken to the cardiac ICU.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the day of your surgery, you'll be admitted to the pre-operative area. The nursing team will start your IV lines, give you antibiotics, and attach monitoring equipment. The anaesthesiologist will place additional monitoring lines and gently put you to sleep with general anaesthesia. A breathing tube will be inserted to support your breathing. You won't feel or remember any of this.

While you're asleep, your surgical team harvests the healthy blood vessels for your bypasses. The internal mammary artery is taken from inside your chest wall, and the saphenous vein from your leg — sometimes using a minimally invasive technique with a small camera (endoscopic harvesting) for a smaller scar. Your surgeon then opens your chest through the breastbone (sternum) and examines your heart and the target arteries.

If you're having on-pump surgery, you'll be connected to the heart-lung machine, which takes over pumping your blood while your heart is temporarily stopped. Your surgeon connects the grafts to your coronary arteries beyond the blockages and to your aorta. If you're having off-pump surgery, stabilising devices hold each target artery still while your surgeon works on your beating heart. Once all grafts are in place, your heart is restarted, the heart-lung machine is weaned off, and your surgeon confirms blood is flowing well through your new bypasses. Temporary pacing wires and chest drainage tubes are placed, your breastbone is closed with stainless steel wires, and your skin is closed with sutures.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

When you first wake up, you'll be in the cardiac ICU, connected to monitors that continuously track your heart rhythm, blood pressure, and overall condition. You'll likely still have the breathing tube in place — this is normal and usually stays for 4 to 8 hours until you're fully awake and can breathe on your own.

**Will it hurt?** Yes, there will be some discomfort — this is major surgery. You'll feel soreness in your chest, and possibly in your leg or arm where the graft vessels were taken. But the pain is very manageable with medication, and it improves significantly within the first few days. You'll receive both IV and oral pain medications.

Once the breathing tube is removed, you'll be encouraged to sit up, cough, and use a breathing device called a spirometer to keep your lungs clear. Your chest tubes are usually removed on the first or second day. Getting moving early is one of the most important parts of your recovery — within 24 to 48 hours, nurses and physiotherapists will help you sit, stand, and walk short distances.

You'll typically transfer from the ICU to the cardiac ward on the second or third day. There, you'll gradually increase your walking distance, practise climbing stairs, and learn how to care for your wounds and manage your medications. Most patients go home or to their nearby accommodation 5 to 7 days after surgery.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

You're probably wondering about the long-term picture — will this surgery work, and how long will the benefits last? The answers are reassuring.

Heart bypass surgery is one of the most thoroughly studied operations in medicine. In experienced centres, the overall survival rate for elective CABG is excellent, with in-hospital mortality around 1 to 2% — and Bangalore's leading hospitals report outcomes consistent with the best international benchmarks. For patients with multi-vessel disease and diabetes, CABG provides better long-term survival than angioplasty, with 5-year survival rates exceeding 90% and 10-year survival rates of approximately 80 to 85%. Most patients experience complete or near-complete relief of chest pain and significant improvement in their ability to exercise and enjoy daily life.

However, bypass surgery is not a cure for coronary artery disease. Over time, your grafts can develop new blockages — particularly the vein grafts from your leg, which have a patency rate of about 50 to 60% at 10 years (compared to over 90% for the internal mammary artery). Long-term success depends on managing your blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes carefully, and stopping smoking. You'll likely be prescribed medications — statins, blood thinners, beta-blockers, and ACE inhibitors — that you'll take indefinitely.

Share this with your cardiologist back home. A follow-up echocardiogram and cardiology review are typically scheduled at 4 to 6 weeks after surgery, with regular cardiac check-ups at least once a year.

Taking your medications exactly as prescribed is critical. You'll typically be on aspirin, a statin, a beta-blocker, and possibly an ACE inhibitor or ARB. Don't stop any medication without talking to your cardiologist first.

## Alternatives to Consider

You might be wondering if there's a less invasive option than open-heart surgery. That depends on your specific situation.

If you have **single or double-vessel disease** with straightforward blockages, angioplasty with stent placement may be a good alternative. Stenting is less invasive, requires a shorter hospital stay (often just 1 to 2 days), and has a faster recovery time. However, for patients with complex, multi-vessel disease — especially if you have diabetes, left main disease, or diffuse blockages — CABG generally provides better long-term outcomes.

If you're not fit for surgery due to advanced age, severe other health problems, or frailty, **optimal medical therapy** may be the most appropriate path. Modern medications can effectively control symptoms and reduce the risk of cardiac events in carefully selected patients.

In some cases, a **hybrid approach** combining stenting with minimally invasive bypass surgery may be considered for complex anatomy. This is available at select Bangalore centres with hybrid operating theatres.
