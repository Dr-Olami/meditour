## Overview

Being told your bone marrow has stopped working properly is terrifying. You may have been feeling exhausted, getting infections easily, bruising without explanation — and then a blood test and bone marrow biopsy confirmed aplastic anaemia. You are probably full of questions: What does this mean? Can it be cured? How long will treatment take? This guide is here to help you understand your options in Bangalore, so you can make informed decisions with confidence rather than fear.

Aplastic anaemia is a rare and serious condition in which your bone marrow fails to produce enough red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets. This leads to fatigue, infections and bleeding. It may be acquired (often immune-mediated, where your body's immune system attacks its own marrow) or inherited. The condition can arise at any age and may be triggered by exposure to certain chemicals, medications, viral infections or radiation — though in many cases the underlying cause remains unknown.

Treatment ranges from supportive care (transfusions and antibiotics) to immunosuppressive therapy with antithymocyte globulin (ATG) and cyclosporine, and ultimately a curative allogeneic stem cell transplant for eligible patients. Bangalore's JCI-accredited hospitals offer both options with experienced haematology teams, HEPA-filtered rooms, 24/7 transfusion services and multidisciplinary support. You will save 70–90% compared to Western pricing, with no compromise in clinical standards.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

Aplastic anaemia treatment in India is designed for you if you have been diagnosed with severe or very severe aplastic anaemia confirmed by bone marrow biopsy showing marked hypocellularity. You typically present with critically low blood counts and face life-threatening risks of infection and bleeding without definitive treatment.

If you are younger (generally under 40) and have an HLA-matched sibling donor, you are the strongest candidate for an upfront curative transplant. This approach offers the best chance of long-term disease-free survival and avoids the uncertainty of whether immunosuppressive therapy will work. If you are older or do not have a matched donor, immunosuppressive therapy combining ATG and cyclosporine is typically recommended, which can achieve meaningful responses in 60–70% of patients. You must have adequate cardiac, renal and hepatic function and no active uncontrolled infection at the time of admission.

## Pre-Operative Preparation

Before your treatment begins, you will undergo a comprehensive diagnostic workup including a detailed bone marrow biopsy with cytogenetics, flow cytometry and molecular testing to confirm the diagnosis and rule out inherited bone marrow failure syndromes. Blood tests assess your complete blood counts, liver and kidney function, viral serology and iron studies.

For transplant candidates, HLA typing of you and your potential sibling donors is performed using high-resolution molecular techniques. If no matched sibling is identified, an unrelated donor search through international registries can be initiated. Your donor undergoes a medical evaluation, infectious disease screening and stem cell collection planning. You will be counselled extensively about the risks, expected timeline and potential complications. Fertility preservation may be discussed if you are young. ## Procedure Details

Your treatment will be tailored to your severity and donor availability. For non-severe disease, supportive care with transfusions and growth factors may suffice. For severe or very severe aplastic anaemia, the two main definitive treatments are immunosuppressive therapy and allogeneic stem cell transplant.

**Immunosuppressive therapy** combines horse or rabbit ATG with cyclosporine over several days of inpatient care, followed by months of outpatient monitoring. ATG is administered intravenously over 4 days with premedication to reduce infusion reactions, and cyclosporine is continued orally for at least 6 months. Response is typically assessed at 3–6 months. If you do not respond, you may be considered for a second course or a salvage transplant.

**Allogeneic stem cell transplant** is the preferred and curative option if you are younger (under 40) and have an HLA-matched sibling donor. The procedure begins with conditioning chemotherapy (typically cyclophosphamide-based, sometimes with ATG or fludarabine), followed by infusion of donor stem cells and engraftment within 10–21 days. The overall inpatient stay spans 4–6 weeks in a HEPA-filtered transplant unit, followed by several weeks of close outpatient monitoring. Throughout either pathway, you will receive supportive care including blood product transfusions, broad-spectrum antibiotics during neutropenia, and nutritional support.

## What Happens During the Procedure

If you are undergoing an allogeneic stem cell transplant, you will be admitted to a dedicated transplant unit and a central venous catheter will be placed. Conditioning chemotherapy is administered over several days — typically cyclophosphamide with or without ATG — to suppress your immune system and prepare your bone marrow to accept the donor cells. During this phase, you will be closely monitored for chemotherapy-related side effects including nausea, mucositis and low blood counts.

Once conditioning is complete, your donor's stem cells are infused intravenously through your central line, similar to a blood transfusion. The infusion takes 30–60 minutes, and the cells naturally home to your bone marrow where they begin to engraft and produce new blood cells. Over the next 10–21 days, you will remain in the transplant unit under strict infection control precautions, with daily blood counts, vital signs monitoring and prophylactic medications.

If you are receiving immunosuppressive therapy instead, ATG is administered over 4 consecutive days with close monitoring for allergic reactions, serum sickness and fever. Cyclosporine is started concurrently and continued orally. You will remain hospitalised during ATG administration and for several days afterwards for observation.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

After discharge from the transplant unit, you will remain in Bangalore for 2–4 weeks of close outpatient follow-up. Your blood counts will be checked 2–3 times per week, immunosuppressive medications adjusted, and any signs of infection or graft-versus-host disease promptly addressed. You should wear a mask in public, avoid crowded places and follow strict food and water safety precautions.

Your immune system takes 6–12 months to fully reconstitute after an allogeneic transplant, and during this time you remain vulnerable to viral, fungal and bacterial infections. Prophylactic medications are continued for several months, and you will be monitored for cytomegalovirus reactivation through regular PCR testing. If you received immunosuppressive therapy, your recovery involves ongoing cyclosporine with gradual tapering as response is confirmed. You will receive detailed discharge instructions, a medication schedule and a written treatment summary for your local haematologist at home.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

Bangalore transplant centres report long-term survival rates of 80–90% for younger patients with severe aplastic anaemia who receive an HLA-matched sibling donor transplant, comparable to international benchmarks. Outcomes are best if you are under 20 with no significant comorbidities and an adequate donor cell dose. Graft failure rates are low at 5–10% in matched sibling donor settings.

Immunosuppressive therapy with ATG and cyclosporine achieves haematological response in approximately 60–70% of patients, with most responses evident by 3–6 months. However, relapse occurs in up to 30–40% of responders over time, and there is a long-term risk of clonal evolution to myelodysplastic syndrome or leukaemia in 10–15% of patients over 10 years. These risks necessitate lifelong haematology surveillance. Most transplant recipients return to full activity within 6–12 months.

## Follow-Up Care After Returning Home

After returning home, you will continue weekly blood count checks with a local haematologist for the first 2–3 months, transitioning to monthly checks as your counts stabilise. Your Bangalore treating team provides a comprehensive discharge summary and remains available for tele-consultation to guide your local physician on medication adjustments, infection management and any concerns.

For transplant patients, immunosuppressive medications are gradually tapered over 6–12 months under joint supervision of your local haematologist and the Bangalore team. Vaccinations are re-administered on a structured schedule starting 6–12 months post-transplant, as your immune system needs to be rebuilt from the donor cells. Annual or biannual visits to Bangalore may be recommended for comprehensive reassessment, particularly during the first 2 years.

## Alternatives to Consider

If you are not a candidate for an allogeneic transplant or lack a matched donor, immunosuppressive therapy remains the standard alternative and can be highly effective. Eltrombopag, a thrombopoietin receptor agonist, has been added to immunosuppressive regimens in recent years and has improved response rates, particularly in patients with very severe aplastic anaemia.

Haploidentical (half-matched) transplants from family members are increasingly offered at experienced centres if you do not have a fully matched donor, using post-transplant cyclophosphamide to reduce graft-versus-host disease. Unrelated donor transplants through international registries are another option, though the search process can take several months and the cost is higher. You should discuss all available options with your treating haematologist to determine the most appropriate pathway based on your age, donor availability, disease severity and personal circumstances.
