## Overview

If your doctor has recommended a bone marrow transplant, you may be feeling overwhelmed by the scale of what lies ahead. That's completely natural. This is one of the most intensive treatments in medicine, and it's normal to have a head full of questions — about what will happen to your body, how long you'll be away from home, and whether it will work.

A bone marrow transplant (BMT) replaces your damaged or diseased bone marrow with healthy blood-forming stem cells. It's used to treat serious blood conditions including leukaemia, lymphoma, multiple myeloma, aplastic anaemia, thalassemia and sickle cell disease. The transplant allows you to receive high-dose chemotherapy and then rebuild a healthy blood and immune system — offering the best realistic chance of a long-term cure for many of these conditions.

There are two main types. In an autologous transplant, your own stem cells are collected, frozen and returned to you after high-dose chemotherapy. In an allogeneic transplant, stem cells come from a donor — usually an HLA-matched sibling, an unrelated volunteer, or a partially matched family member. The right choice depends on your specific disease, its stage, whether a suitable donor is available, and your overall health.

India has become a leading destination for bone marrow transplants, with JCI-accredited hospitals in Bangalore offering comprehensive transplant programmes at a fraction of what you'd pay in the West. The transplant units are equipped with HEPA-filtered rooms, laminar airflow systems, dedicated apheresis units and on-site stem cell freezing facilities. Many Bangalore hospitals also work with international donor databases, which means more donor options if you don't have a matched sibling.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

A bone marrow transplant may be recommended for you if you have a confirmed blood cancer such as acute leukaemia, high-risk lymphoma or multiple myeloma — particularly if your disease has relapsed after standard therapy or carries high-risk genetic features. You'll need to be in remission or have minimal disease burden before proceeding.

The procedure is also an option for non-malignant conditions including thalassemia major, severe aplastic anaemia and sickle cell disease. In these cases, the transplant replaces your defective marrow with healthy donor cells, offering a cure rather than lifelong symptom management. If your child has thalassemia or sickle cell disease and you have an HLA-matched sibling donor, they're a particularly strong candidate.

You'll need adequate heart, kidney and liver function to tolerate conditioning chemotherapy and the prolonged marrow suppression that follows. Any active infections must be resolved before admission. You and your family also need to be psychologically and logistically prepared for a prolonged stay of 8–12 weeks, with the possibility of extensions if complications arise.

## Procedure Details

Your transplant journey begins with a thorough evaluation — blood tests, imaging, heart and lung assessments, and donor matching (HLA typing) if you're having an allogeneic transplant. This phase ensures you're medically fit to handle the intensive conditioning regimen. If you're having an allogeneic transplant, your donor will also undergo HLA typing, infectious disease screening and a full medical check.

Once you're cleared, you'll receive conditioning chemotherapy (and sometimes total body irradiation) over several days. This destroys your diseased marrow and suppresses your immune system. The regimen may be high-intensity (myeloablative) or reduced-intensity, depending on your age, other health conditions and diagnosis. During conditioning, you may experience nausea, mouth sores, fatigue and diarrhoea — all of which your team will manage with supportive medications.

Then comes the stem cell infusion, which is given through your vein much like a blood transfusion. These cells travel to your bone marrow cavities and begin producing new blood cells — a process called engraftment that typically takes 10–21 days. During this vulnerable period you'll stay in a specialised transplant room with strict infection control measures, receiving antibiotics, blood transfusions and nutritional support.

If you're having an allogeneic transplant, you'll also receive immunosuppressive medications to prevent graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) — a complication where donor immune cells attack your tissues. Your team will watch closely for early signs of acute GVHD, including skin rashes, liver problems and gastrointestinal symptoms. The entire process spans 4–8 weeks of inpatient care, followed by several weeks of close outpatient monitoring.

## What Happens During the Procedure

When you're admitted, you'll be settled into a HEPA-filtered transplant room and a central venous catheter (Hickman or PICC line) will be inserted under local anaesthesia or sedation. This line provides reliable intravenous access for your chemotherapy, stem cell infusion, blood transfusions and medications throughout the transplant period.

Conditioning chemotherapy begins the following day and is given over 2 to 7 days, depending on your regimen. Each drug goes through your central line, with close monitoring for allergic reactions and organ toxicity. You'll receive anti-nausea medications, hydration and supportive care. If your regimen includes total body irradiation, you'll attend the radiotherapy department for precisely calculated doses over 1 to 3 days.

After a rest period of 1 to 2 days, your stem cell infusion takes place. Cryopreserved or freshly collected donor stem cells are infused through your central line over 30 to 60 minutes. The infusion is generally painless, though you may notice a taste of garlic or some flushing due to the cryoprotectant (DMSO) — this is completely normal. Over the next 10 to 21 days, you'll remain in your transplant room while the infused stem cells migrate to your bone marrow and begin producing new blood cells. Your blood counts will drop to their lowest levels, and you'll be highly susceptible to infections and bleeding. You'll receive antibiotics, antifungals and antivirals preventively, with blood and platelet transfusions as needed. Once your neutrophil counts rise sustainably for three consecutive days, engraftment is declared — a major milestone in your recovery.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

In the days immediately after engraftment, you'll remain in hospital so your team can monitor for early complications. The focus shifts to managing mouth sores, watching for acute GVHD (if you've had an allogeneic transplant), and providing pain management and nutritional support. You'll be encouraged to get up and move around gently within your room to prevent muscle loss and blood clots.

Once you're discharged, you'll move to outpatient monitoring with visits two to three times per week. Your blood counts, liver and kidney function, immunosuppressive drug levels and infection markers will be checked at each visit. You'll need to wear a mask in public, avoid crowds and practise strict hand hygiene. Dietary restrictions continue with a neutropenic diet until your immune system fully recovers.

**Will it hurt?** The transplant itself isn't painful, but conditioning chemotherapy can cause significant discomfort — mouth sores, nausea, fatigue and general weakness. Your team will manage these with medications, and they do gradually improve. The emotional toll can be just as real: prolonged isolation and uncertainty can affect your mental health. Many Bangalore hospitals offer counselling services and can connect you with support groups of former transplant recipients.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

Long-term survival after a bone marrow transplant in Bangalore is comparable to international benchmarks, with overall survival rates of 60–90% depending on your underlying disease, donor match quality, your age and your condition at the time of transplant. Autologous transplants for lymphoma and myeloma generally have lower transplant-related mortality (1–5%) but carry a risk of relapse. Allogeneic transplants have higher transplant-related mortality (10–25%) but offer a graft-versus-leukaemia effect that reduces relapse risk.

For non-malignant conditions such as thalassemia and sickle cell disease, disease-free survival rates of 85–95% are reported in children with matched sibling donors. Your quality of life will improve progressively over 6 to 12 months as your immune system rebuilds. Most people return to work or school within 9 to 12 months. Long-term follow-up focuses on surveillance for relapse, chronic GVHD, endocrine dysfunction, fertility issues and secondary malignancies.

## Follow-Up Care After Returning Home

Once you're back home, you'll need to establish care with a local haematologist for weekly blood counts during your first month, gradually reducing the frequency as your counts stabilise. Your Bangalore transplant team will provide a detailed discharge summary for you to share with your local physician, and tele-consultation with your treating haematologist is available through Khan Meditour for the first year.

Taking your medications exactly as prescribed is critical — particularly if you've had an allogeneic transplant, as you'll need to continue immunosuppressive medications for 6 to 12 months, with regular blood level monitoring. Infection prophylaxis with antiviral, antifungal and antibacterial medications is typically continued for 3 to 6 months. You should seek immediate medical attention if you develop a fever above 38°C, unusual bleeding, severe diarrhoea, jaundice, new skin rashes or persistent headaches. Annual return visits to Bangalore for comprehensive reassessment are recommended for the first two to three years.

## Alternatives to Consider

If you're not a candidate for a transplant or don't have a suitable donor, there are other options. For blood cancers, continued chemotherapy with novel agents, targeted therapy or immunotherapy may achieve disease control without the risks of transplant. If you have chronic myeloid leukaemia, tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as imatinib provide excellent long-term control without transplant in most people.

For non-malignant conditions such as thalassemia and sickle cell disease, lifelong transfusion and chelation therapy remains a viable option, though it doesn't offer a cure and carries long-term risks of iron overload and organ damage. Gene therapy is an emerging alternative that uses your own genetically modified stem cells, potentially eliminating the need for a donor and the risk of GVHD — though availability is still limited and costs are high.

If you're older or have significant other health conditions, a reduced-intensity allogeneic transplant may offer a middle ground. The decision between transplant and alternative therapies should be made together with your treating haematologist.
