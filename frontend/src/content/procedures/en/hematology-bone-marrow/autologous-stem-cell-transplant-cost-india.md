## Overview

If your doctor has recommended an autologous stem cell transplant, you may be feeling anxious about what lies ahead. That is completely understandable. The good news is that this type of transplant uses your own stem cells — meaning there is no donor matching needed and no risk of graft-versus-host disease. This makes the procedure safer and your recovery faster than an allogeneic transplant, while still giving you the best chance of long-term disease control.

An autologous stem cell transplant is a procedure in which your own blood-forming stem cells are collected, frozen and reinfused after high-dose chemotherapy. It is primarily used to treat multiple myeloma, relapsed or high-risk Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and selected solid tumours such as neuroblastoma. The high-dose conditioning destroys cancer cells, and your stored stem cells rescue your bone marrow from otherwise lethal toxicity.

Bangalore's JCI-accredited hospitals perform thousands of autologous transplants each year with outcomes comparable to international benchmarks. You will find state-of-the-art apheresis machines, cryopreservation facilities and dedicated HEPA-filtered transplant units that meet global safety standards. The cost savings of 70–90% compared to Western pricing make this life-saving procedure accessible when it might otherwise be financially out of reach.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

An autologous stem cell transplant may be recommended for you if you have multiple myeloma and are transplant-eligible — typically under 65–70 years of age with adequate organ function and a good performance status. It is also indicated if you have relapsed or high-risk Hodgkin or non-Hodgkin lymphoma and have achieved at least a partial response to salvage chemotherapy. In selected paediatric cases, it is used for solid tumours such as neuroblastoma.

You must have an adequate stem cell yield from mobilisation and apheresis. If you have received extensive prior therapy, your stem cell mobilisation may be poor, and alternative strategies or bone marrow harvest may be considered. You need adequate cardiac, renal and hepatic function to tolerate the high-dose conditioning, and a performance status of 0–2 on the ECOG scale. If you have severe comorbidities such as uncontrolled heart failure, severe lung disease or active uncontrolled infection, you may not be a candidate.

## Pre-Operative Preparation

Your pre-transplant workup includes blood tests (complete blood count, liver and kidney function, viral serology), imaging (PET-CT or MRI as appropriate to your disease), a bone marrow biopsy to confirm disease status, and cardiac and pulmonary function tests. These evaluations ensure you are fit enough to tolerate high-dose chemotherapy and the subsequent period of neutropenia.

You and your family will be counselled about the procedure, expected timeline, potential complications and the importance of infection prevention during the neutropenic phase. A central venous catheter will be placed for both apheresis and your transplant admission. ## Procedure Details

Your transplant journey begins with stem cell mobilisation — you will receive chemotherapy and/or growth factors (G-CSF) to push stem cells from your bone marrow into your bloodstream. These cells are then collected by apheresis over 1–3 days, with each session lasting 3–4 hours. The collected cells are cryopreserved in a GMP-certified laboratory until reinfusion. A minimum yield of 2×10⁶ CD34+ cells/kg is required, though many centres aim for 4–5×10⁶ to ensure rapid engraftment.

Once collection is complete, you will be admitted for conditioning — high-dose chemotherapy administered over several days. For myeloma, single-agent melphalan is standard. For lymphoma, the BEAM regimen (carmustine, etoposide, cytarabine, melphalan) is commonly used. Your frozen stem cells are then thawed and reinfused intravenously, much like a blood transfusion. Engraftment typically occurs within 10–14 days, during which you will stay in a specialised transplant room with strict infection control measures.

The overall procedure spans 2–4 weeks of inpatient care, followed by several weeks of close outpatient monitoring. Because you receive your own cells, there is no risk of graft-versus-host disease. However, you will still experience a period of profound neutropenia during which you are vulnerable to infections, and supportive care including antibiotics, antifungals, antivirals and blood product transfusions is essential.

## What Happens During the Procedure

After admission to the transplant unit and placement of your central venous catheter, you will receive high-dose conditioning chemotherapy over 1–4 days depending on your regimen. During conditioning, you will be monitored for chemotherapy-related side effects including nausea, mucositis and organ toxicity. Your team will manage these with supportive medications.

On the day of reinfusion, your cryopreserved stem cells are thawed in the laboratory and brought to your room. The cells are infused intravenously through your central line over 30–60 minutes. You may experience a transient taste or smell from the cryoprotectant DMSO, and some flushing or mild hypotension during infusion — this is completely normal and your vital signs will be monitored throughout.

Over the next 10–14 days, you will remain in the transplant unit while waiting for engraftment. During this neutropenic phase, your blood counts will be checked daily, prophylactic antibiotics and antifungals administered, and any fever treated promptly with broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics. Blood and platelet transfusions are given as needed. Once engraftment occurs (neutrophils above 0.5×10⁹/L for 3 consecutive days), you will transition to outpatient monitoring.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

After discharge from the transplant unit, you will remain in Bangalore for 2–3 weeks of close outpatient follow-up. Your blood counts will be checked 2–3 times per week, and any signs of infection, bleeding or organ dysfunction promptly addressed. You should wear a mask in public, avoid crowded places, practise strict hand hygiene and follow food and water safety precautions. Nutritional support will help you regain weight and strength lost during the inpatient phase.

Your immune system takes 3–6 months to fully recover. During this time, you remain vulnerable to infections, particularly viral reactivations such as herpes zoster. Prophylactic antiviral medications are continued for several months, and vaccination updates are scheduled once your immune recovery is confirmed. Fatigue is common during the first 2–3 months and gradually improves. Most people return to work and normal activities within 3–6 months, though the exact timeline depends on your underlying disease, response to transplant and any ongoing maintenance therapy.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

Leading Bangalore transplant centres report progression-free survival rates of 50–80% at five years depending on your underlying disease and remission status at the time of transplant. For multiple myeloma, autologous transplant combined with novel-agent induction and maintenance therapy achieves median progression-free survival of 36–60 months, with overall survival exceeding 80% at five years in transplant-eligible patients.

For relapsed Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, autologous transplant achieves long-term remission in 40–60% of patients who are in chemosensitive relapse. Outcomes are significantly better if you achieve a complete or very good partial response to salvage chemotherapy before transplant. Long-term survivors are monitored for disease relapse, secondary malignancies and late effects of chemotherapy including cardiac toxicity, infertility and neuropathy.

After returning home, you will continue weekly blood count checks with a local haematologist for the first 1–2 months, transitioning to monthly checks as your counts stabilise. If you have myeloma, maintenance therapy with lenalidomide or bortezomib is typically continued long-term under the supervision of your local haematologist, with periodic reassessment visits to Bangalore. Vaccinations are updated on a structured schedule starting 3–6 months post-transplant. ## Alternatives to Consider

If you are not a candidate for autologous transplant due to poor stem cell mobilisation, organ dysfunction or advanced age, continued standard-dose therapy with novel agents may be your best option. In myeloma, this includes combinations of proteasome inhibitors, immunomodulatory drugs and monoclonal antibodies administered in cycles without the need for high-dose conditioning.

If your lymphoma has relapsed after an autologous transplant, an allogeneic stem cell transplant may be considered as a salvage strategy. CAR-T cell therapy is another emerging option for relapsed B-cell lymphoma or leukaemia. Bangalore centres offer both allogeneic transplant and CAR-T therapy. You should discuss all available alternatives with your treating haematologist to determine the most appropriate pathway based on your disease type, response to prior therapy, age, fitness and personal circumstances.
