## Overview

If you or your child has sickle cell disease, you know the pain and exhaustion that come with every crisis. You may have watched the unpredictable pain episodes, the hospital admissions and the worry about what the next month will bring. You may also have been told that a bone marrow transplant is the only real cure — and then discovered how difficult and expensive that can be to access in your own country. This page is here to help you understand your options in India, clearly and honestly.

Sickle cell disease is a condition you were born with. It changes the shape of your red blood cells so they become rigid and block small blood vessels, causing pain, anaemia and, over time, damage to organs like your spleen, kidneys and brain. Treatment ranges from daily medicines that reduce how often crises happen, to a bone marrow or stem cell transplant that can actually cure the disease by replacing your body's blood-forming cells with healthy ones from a donor.

Bangalore has become one of the most trusted destinations in the world for sickle cell care. Hospitals here offer everything from crisis management to full curative transplants, using the same protocols and equipment you would find in leading Western centres — but at 70–90% lower cost. Whether you are travelling from Bangladesh, the Gulf countries, East Africa or elsewhere, you will find experienced haematology teams, English-speaking staff and dedicated international patient services ready to support you and your family through every step.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

This treatment is suitable for a wide range of people living with sickle cell disease. If you or your child has confirmed HbSS, HbSβ-thalassemia or another sickle genotype and you continue to have pain crises despite taking hydroxyurea, you are a strong candidate for evaluation. If you have already had a stroke, acute chest syndrome or signs of progressive organ damage, your doctor may recommend a curative transplant more urgently — provided a suitable donor can be found.

For a bone marrow transplant, the ideal candidate is a child or young adult with an HLA-matched sibling donor, good organ function and no active infection or crisis at the time of admission. If you do not have a matched sibling, you may still be considered using an unrelated donor from international registries or a half-matched (haploidentical) family donor, though these carry higher risks. If you are older or have significant organ damage already, your team may advise that optimised medical management is actually the safer and more effective path for you. A thorough evaluation once you arrive in Bangalore will help determine the right approach for your specific situation.

## Pre-Operative Preparation

Your preparation begins at home. Before you travel, gather all your recent medical records — your haemoglobin electrophoresis results, transfusion history, any brain MRI or MRA scans, and your latest organ function tests. These will be shared with the Bangalore transplant team so they can give you a remote opinion and confirm whether a transplant is the right step. Once you arrive, you will undergo a comprehensive in-person workup over five to seven days. This includes a heart check with echocardiography, kidney and liver function panels, infection screening and detailed HLA typing of you and your potential donors.

Your donor will also go through a full medical assessment to make sure they are fit and healthy enough for stem cell collection. During this time, your team will sit down with you and your family to explain the conditioning regimen, what side effects to expect and what the timeline will look like. Your vaccinations will be updated if needed, and a central venous catheter will be planned to give your team reliable access to your veins during the transplant. Psychological support is available for both you and your family, because this journey is physically and emotionally demanding — and it is perfectly normal to feel anxious about what lies ahead.

## Procedure Details

Your treatment plan will be built around how severe your disease is and what you hope to achieve. If your crises are manageable, your doctor may focus on supportive care — folic acid supplements, plenty of fluids, pain relief during episodes and a medicine called hydroxyurea that helps your body produce a type of haemoglobin that resists sickling. Regular blood transfusions may be recommended if you have had a stroke or are at risk of one. Newer drugs like crizanlizumab and voxelotor may also be discussed if standard therapy is not controlling your symptoms well enough.

If you are looking for a cure, an allogeneic stem cell transplant is the only established option that can eliminate sickle cell disease entirely. The process starts with testing your brothers or sisters to see if one is a close enough match to be your donor. Once a match is found, you will receive conditioning chemotherapy to prepare your body, and then healthy stem cells from your donor are infused through a vein — much like a blood transfusion. Over the next two to three weeks, those cells settle into your bone marrow and begin producing normal red blood cells that do not sickle. You will stay in a specially filtered hospital room during this time, with your blood counts checked every day. The best results are seen in children who have a matched sibling donor and have not yet had a stroke.

## What Happens During the Procedure

If you are having a curative transplant, you will be admitted to a HEPA-filtered transplant unit — a specially filtered room that keeps the air clean to protect you while your immune system is low. You will begin conditioning chemotherapy, which typically lasts five to seven days. This chemotherapy suppresses your own bone marrow to make space for the donor cells and reduces the risk of your body rejecting them. The specific regimen — whether full-intensity or reduced-intensity — will be chosen based on your age, your organ function and your overall health.

Once conditioning is complete, your donor's stem cells are infused through your central line, much like a blood transfusion. The infusion itself takes only 30 to 60 minutes. You will not feel anything dramatic during it. Over the following 10 to 21 days, the donor cells travel to your bone marrow and begin producing healthy red blood cells that do not sickle. During this engraftment period, you will be monitored closely for infections, bleeding and a complication called graft-versus-host disease, where the donor cells can sometimes react against your own tissues. Your blood counts will be checked daily and you may need supportive transfusions. Once engraftment is confirmed and you are clinically stable, you will move to outpatient monitoring.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

After you are discharged from the transplant unit, you will remain in Bangalore for two to four weeks of outpatient follow-up. Your blood counts will be checked twice a week, and your immunosuppressive medications will be adjusted based on how well your new cells are engrafting and whether there are any signs of graft-versus-host disease. You will continue taking antiviral, antifungal and antibacterial medications for several months to protect you from infection. Your team will ask you to avoid crowds, wear a mask in public and maintain strict hand hygiene — simple habits that make a big difference during this vulnerable period.

You will gradually rebuild your physical activity, starting with short walks and increasing over the weeks. A dietitian will guide your nutrition to support your immune recovery and healing. Most people feel significantly better within four to six weeks as normal haemoglobin production resumes and sickle-related symptoms fade. Before you are cleared to fly home — typically around week six to ten — your treating team will give you a detailed discharge summary and a clear medication schedule so you and your local doctor know exactly what to do next.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

Bangalore transplant centres report disease-free survival rates of 85–95% in children with an HLA-matched sibling donor, which is consistent with the best international results. A successful transplant eliminates sickle-related symptoms — the pain crises, the anaemia, the stroke risk — and dramatically improves quality of life. Most people no longer need transfusions or hydroxyurea once their new cells are stable.

That said, it is important to be honest about the longer-term picture. You will need ongoing monitoring for graft-versus-host disease, which can affect your skin, gut or liver. Conditioning chemotherapy can also have effects on growth and fertility, and these will be discussed with you openly before you commit to the procedure. Regular follow-up with a haematologist near your home is essential for at least the first year. Adult recipients may have somewhat lower survival rates because of existing organ damage, but outcomes remain strong for well-chosen candidates. Gene therapy is a promising future option, but it is not yet standard care and is currently available only through clinical trials at very high cost.

## Follow-Up Care After Returning Home

Once you are back home, the most important step is to establish care with a local haematologist who is familiar with post-transplant management. You will need weekly blood counts for the first month, then gradually reduce to monthly and eventually quarterly checks. Your immunosuppressive medications will be tapered under guidance from your Bangalore team, who remain available for tele-consultation whenever you or your local doctor need them. Your vaccinations will be restarted on a modified schedule beginning around six months after your transplant, because your new immune system needs time to respond properly.

You should contact your doctor immediately if you develop a fever, rash, jaundice or persistent fatigue — these can be early signs of infection or graft-versus-host disease, and catching them early makes a real difference. Annual check-ups will assess your organ function, iron levels and donor chimerism (the proportion of donor cells in your blood) to confirm that your cure is holding. Your Bangalore transplant coordinator will stay in contact with you for at least a year to help ensure a smooth transition back to care in your home country.

## Alternatives to Consider

If a transplant is not the right path for you — or while you are waiting for a donor to be found — optimised medical management can still be highly effective. Hydroxyurea, regular transfusion programmes and newer drugs like crizanlizumab and voxelotor can significantly reduce how often you have crises and help protect your organs from damage. For many people, these therapies make a meaningful difference to daily life.

Gene therapy is an exciting emerging alternative that uses your own modified stem cells, which would eliminate the need for a donor altogether. However, it is currently available only through clinical trials and at very high cost in Western centres, so it is not a practical option for most families today. The best thing you can do is discuss all your options openly with the Bangalore haematology team. They will give you a balanced, honest recommendation based on how severe your disease is, whether a donor is available, your age and your organ function. In some cases, a trial of intensified medical therapy is the wisest first step before committing to a transplant.

## Travel & Visa

To travel to India for sickle cell treatment, you will need a Medical Visa (e-Medical or regular Medical Visa), which is granted to you and up to two attendants. The visa is usually valid for up to one year with multiple entries, which allows you to come for your initial assessment, return for the transplant and come back for follow-up if needed. You will need a visa invitation letter from the treating hospital, your recent medical records and a passport with at least six months of validity remaining.

You should plan to arrive in Bangalore at least one week before your scheduled transplant date. This gives your medical team time to complete your pre-transplant workup, confirm your donor match and finalise your conditioning plan. Khan Meditour will assist you with the visa invitation letter from your hospital, airport pickup, accommodation for your family near the hospital and coordination of every appointment throughout your stay. One important piece of advice: avoid long-haul flights during an active pain crisis. If you are in crisis, stabilise first and travel once your symptoms have settled. Carry a written summary of your transfusion history and current medications with you on the plane.

What should you pack? Comfortable, loose-fitting clothes, a warm layer for air-conditioned hospital rooms, all your medications in their original packaging, your medical records organised in a single folder, and basic toiletries. If you are travelling with a child, bring a few familiar items from home — a favourite blanket or toy can make a long hospital stay feel less frightening.

Most surgeons recommend waiting at least 7-14 days before taking a long-haul flight, to reduce the risk of deep vein thrombosis and other travel-related complications.
