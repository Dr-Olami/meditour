## Overview

Being told you need an allogeneic stem cell transplant is overwhelming, and you probably have dozens of questions. Let us walk you through what this treatment means in plain, honest language.

An allogeneic stem cell transplant uses healthy blood-forming stem cells from a donor — someone whose tissue type closely matches yours — to replace your diseased bone marrow. Your donor could be a matched sibling, a matched unrelated donor from an international registry, or a half-matched (haploidentical) family member. The donor cells rebuild a healthy blood and immune system and also help hunt down remaining cancer cells — a powerful effect doctors call "graft-versus-leukaemia." This makes the procedure potentially curative for diseases that chemotherapy alone cannot fully control.

If you do not have a fully matched sibling, Bangalore transplant centres can search international registries or use a half-matched family donor. India has become a leading destination for allogeneic transplants, with JCI-accredited Bangalore hospitals offering complete programmes at a fraction of Western costs, equipped with HEPA-filtered rooms, laminar airflow systems, and on-site cryopreservation.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

You may be considering an allogeneic transplant if you have acute myeloid or lymphoblastic leukaemia in first or subsequent complete remission, particularly if high-risk features make relapse more likely with chemotherapy alone. It is also recommended for myelodysplastic syndromes, chronic myeloid leukaemia in accelerated or blast phase, and certain high-risk lymphomas that have come back after previous treatment.

This procedure is not only for blood cancers. If you have severe aplastic anaemia and a matched sibling donor, an allogeneic transplant can offer a cure rather than a lifetime of transfusions. It is also used for inherited bone marrow disorders like thalassemia major, sickle cell disease and immunodeficiency syndromes. If your child has thalassemia or sickle cell disease and has an HLA-matched sibling donor, they are a particularly strong candidate.

To go through this safely, your heart, kidneys and liver need to be functioning well enough to handle the conditioning chemotherapy and prolonged marrow suppression. If you have some organ impairment, you may be offered a reduced-intensity conditioning regimen instead. Any active infections must be fully resolved before admission.

You and your family also need to be emotionally and logistically prepared for a long stay in India — typically 8 to 12 weeks, with the possibility of extensions if complications arise. You will need a committed attendant with you, and you should have a clear, honest understanding of the risks and the long recovery road ahead.

## Procedure Details

Your transplant journey begins with a thorough evaluation — blood tests, imaging scans, heart and lung checks, and HLA typing to identify the best donor. Once your donor is confirmed, they undergo infectious disease screening and stem cell collection via bone marrow harvest or peripheral blood apheresis.

Next comes conditioning — several days of chemotherapy, and sometimes total body irradiation, designed to clear out your diseased marrow and suppress your immune system so your body accepts the donor cells. Depending on your age, health and diagnosis, your doctor will choose either a myeloablative (high-intensity) or reduced-intensity regimen. You may feel nauseous, develop mouth sores, or feel exhausted — your care team will manage these with supportive medications.

After conditioning, the donor stem cells are infused through an IV line, much like a blood transfusion. Those cells travel to your bone marrow cavities and start producing new blood cells — a process called engraftment that typically takes 10 to 21 days. During this vulnerable window, you will stay in a specialised transplant room with strict infection control measures, receiving antibiotics, blood transfusions and immunosuppressive drugs to protect you and prevent GVHD.

The entire inpatient phase spans 4 to 6 weeks, followed by several weeks of close outpatient monitoring. A full team — haematologists, transplant nurses, pharmacists, dietitians and infectious disease specialists — will be with you every step of the way.

## What Happens During the Procedure

When you are admitted, you will be settled into your HEPA-filtered transplant room. A central venous catheter (a Hickman or PICC line) will be inserted under local anaesthesia or light sedation. This thin tube placed into a large vein in your chest or arm means you will not need repeated needle pricks — all your chemotherapy, stem cell infusion, blood transfusions and medications go through this line.

**Will it hurt?** The catheter insertion involves some discomfort but is done with local anaesthetic and sedation. The conditioning chemotherapy is given through the line, so you will not feel it going in, though you may experience side effects like nausea, mouth sores and fatigue. Your anti-nausea medications will be adjusted to keep you comfortable.

Conditioning begins the day after admission and runs for 2 to 7 days. If your plan includes total body irradiation, you will receive precisely calculated doses over 1 to 3 days. After a short rest period, your donor's stem cells are infused through your central line over 30 to 60 minutes. The infusion is painless — some people describe a warm flushing sensation or a strange taste, but that passes quickly.

Over the next 10 to 21 days, you stay in your transplant room while the infused cells find their way to your bone marrow and start producing new blood cells. Your blood counts will drop to their lowest levels, and you are highly vulnerable to infections and bleeding. You will receive antibiotics, antifungals and antivirals preventively, with blood and platelet transfusions as needed. Once your neutrophil counts rise sustainably for three consecutive days, your team will declare engraftment — when your new immune system starts to come alive.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

Once engraftment occurs, you will stay in hospital for several more days while your team watches for early complications. The focus shifts to managing mouth sores, monitoring for acute graft-versus-host disease (which can show up as skin rashes, liver problems or digestive issues), and keeping you comfortable with pain management and nutritional support. You will be encouraged to get up and move around gently within your room to prevent muscle loss and blood clots.

When you are discharged from hospital, you will move to outpatient monitoring with visits two to three times per week. At each visit, your team checks your blood counts, liver and kidney function, immunosuppressive drug levels and infection markers. You will need to wear a mask in public, avoid crowds and practise strict hand hygiene. Your diet will continue to be restricted to a neutropenic diet until your immune system fully recovers.

The psychological side of recovery is just as real as the physical side. Weeks of isolation can take a toll on your mental health, and it is completely normal to feel anxious or low. Many Bangalore hospitals offer counselling services and can connect you with support groups of people who have been through exactly what you are experiencing.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

You deserve an honest picture of what to expect. Long-term survival after an allogeneic stem cell transplant in Bangalore is comparable to international benchmarks, with overall survival rates of 50 to 90% depending on your specific disease, donor match quality, your age and condition at the time of transplant. If you have acute leukaemia in first complete remission with a matched sibling donor, your outlook is among the best — survival rates of 70 to 90%. Unrelated and haploidentical transplants carry slightly higher risks but offer curative potential if you do not have a matched sibling.

Your quality of life will improve progressively over 6 to 12 months as your immune system rebuilds itself. Most people return to work or school within 9 to 12 months. Long-term follow-up focuses on watching for disease relapse, chronic GVHD, endocrine issues, fertility concerns and secondary malignancies. Regular bone marrow biopsies, blood tests and imaging will be part of your long-term monitoring plan — careful surveillance to keep you healthy.

## Follow-Up Care After Returning Home

When you return to your home country, you will need to establish care with a local haematologist for weekly blood counts during your first month back. As your counts stabilise, these visits will gradually become less frequent. Your Bangalore transplant team will provide a detailed discharge summary for your local physician, and tele-consultation with your treating haematologist is available through Khan Meditour for your entire first year.

Taking your medications exactly as prescribed is critical. Immunosuppressive medications (cyclosporine or tacrolimus) must be continued for 6 to 12 months with regular blood level monitoring — do not skip doses or stop on your own. Infection prophylaxis with antiviral, antifungal and antibacterial medications typically continues for 3 to 6 months. If you develop a fever above 38°C, unusual bleeding, severe diarrhoea, jaundice, new skin rashes or persistent headaches, seek medical attention immediately. Annual return visits to Bangalore for a comprehensive reassessment are recommended for the first two to three years.

## Alternatives to Consider

If you are not a candidate for an allogeneic transplant, or if no suitable donor can be found, there are other paths worth discussing with your doctor. For blood cancers, continued chemotherapy with newer targeted drugs or immunotherapy may achieve disease control without the risks of transplant. If you have chronic myeloid leukaemia, tyrosine kinase inhibitors can provide excellent long-term control without a transplant in most cases.

For non-malignant conditions like thalassemia and sickle cell disease, lifelong transfusion and chelation therapy remains a viable option, though it does not offer a cure and carries long-term risks of iron overload and organ damage. Gene therapy is an emerging alternative that uses your own genetically modified stem cells, potentially eliminating the need for a donor and the risk of GVHD — but availability is still limited and costs are high. If you are older or have significant other health conditions, a reduced-intensity allogeneic transplant may offer a middle ground with lower conditioning toxicity.

To travel to India for your transplant, you will need a Medical Visa (e-Medical or regular Medical Visa), which is granted to you and up to two attendants. The visa is typically valid for up to one year with multiple entries, which is important because you may need to return for follow-up visits. The application requires a visa invitation letter from your treating hospital, which Khan Meditour will arrange for you. Processing usually takes 3 to 10 working days.

You should plan to arrive in Bangalore at least one week before your scheduled transplant date. This gives your team time to complete your pre-transplant workup, confirm donor compatibility, perform final imaging and cardiac assessments, and make sure you are free from any active infection. For an allogeneic transplant, your donor must also travel to Bangalore for their medical evaluation and stem cell collection.

**What should you pack?** Bring comfortable, loose-fitting clothes, your medical records organised in a folder, all current medications in their original packaging, personal toiletries, a phone charger with an adapter for Indian sockets, and something comforting from home. You will be in a HEPA-filtered room for much of your stay, so small items that make it feel less clinical can help your mental wellbeing.
