## Overview

Targeted therapy is a precision cancer treatment that uses drugs designed to specifically identify and attack cancer cells based on their unique molecular characteristics. Unlike conventional chemotherapy, which affects all rapidly dividing cells, targeted therapy zeroes in on specific proteins, genes, or signalling pathways that cancer cells rely on for growth and survival. This approach minimises damage to healthy cells and often results in fewer and more manageable side effects.

In India, targeted therapy is delivered in advanced oncology centres with access to comprehensive molecular profiling laboratories. The targeted agents used—including tyrosine kinase inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, and proteasome inhibitors—are sourced from the same global pharmaceutical companies that supply Western hospitals. Treatment is guided by molecular test results and follows internationally recognised protocols from NCCN, ESMO, and other leading oncology guidelines.

The cost of targeted therapy in India ranges from $3,000 to $12,000 per course, representing savings of up to 90% compared to the United States and Europe. This affordability is particularly significant for targeted therapies, which are among the most expensive cancer treatments globally. Indian hospitals offer these cutting-edge drugs at dramatically reduced prices, making precision oncology accessible to international patients.

## Procedure Details

Targeted therapy begins with molecular profiling of the tumour, which involves testing biopsy tissue or blood samples for specific genetic mutations, gene amplifications, or protein expressions. Common targets include EGFR mutations in lung cancer, HER2 amplification in breast and gastric cancer, BRAF mutations in melanoma, and BCR-ABL in chronic myeloid leukaemia. The results of these tests determine which targeted agent is appropriate for each patient.

Once a target is identified, the medical oncologist prescribes the appropriate targeted therapy. Many targeted therapies are oral medications taken daily at home, while others are administered as intravenous infusions in a hospital or clinic setting. For oral therapies, patients receive their initial supply in Bangalore and can continue treatment at home, with periodic follow-up visits for reassessment. For IV therapies, patients attend infusion sessions every 1–3 weeks depending on the drug and protocol.

Monitoring during targeted therapy is essential. Patients undergo regular blood tests to check for side effects such as liver enzyme elevation, cardiac toxicity, or blood count changes. Imaging studies (CT or PET-CT) are performed every 8–12 weeks to assess tumour response. Side effects are generally milder than chemotherapy but can include skin rashes, diarrhoea, fatigue, and hypertension, which are managed with supportive medications and dose adjustments as needed.
