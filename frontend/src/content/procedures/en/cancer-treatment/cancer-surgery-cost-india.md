## Overview

If you or someone you love has just received a cancer diagnosis, you are likely feeling overwhelmed and full of questions. You may be wondering whether surgery is the right path, whether you will be in pain, how long recovery will take, and whether this treatment is meant to cure the cancer or to ease your symptoms. These are completely natural concerns, and you deserve clear, honest answers before you make any decisions.

Cancer surgery—sometimes called surgical oncology—is the physical removal of a tumour and the tissue surrounding it. For many solid tumours that have not spread beyond their original location, surgery offers the strongest chance of long-term control or cure. Your surgeon may recommend surgery on its own, or as part of a combined plan that also includes chemotherapy or radiation therapy. The approach is tailored to your specific cancer, your overall health, and your personal goals.

Bangalore's leading oncology hospitals offer the full spectrum of surgical techniques, from traditional open surgery to minimally invasive laparoscopic and robotic-assisted procedures. The surgical oncologists who will care for you have trained at premier institutions in India and abroad, and they work within multidisciplinary tumour boards—meaning your case is reviewed by surgical, medical, and radiation oncologists together, so every angle is considered before a plan is finalised.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

Cancer surgery may be right for you if imaging studies confirm that your tumour is resectable—meaning it can be safely removed. If your cancer is localised or locally advanced and has not spread to distant organs, surgery with curative intent is often the recommended path. Common cancers treated surgically include breast, colorectal, lung, prostate, thyroid, gynaecological, and head and neck cancers.

Your overall health matters just as much as the tumour itself. Before recommending surgery, your team will assess your cardiac and pulmonary function, blood counts, and any existing conditions such as diabetes or hypertension through a pre-anaesthetic evaluation. If you have severe heart disease, poorly controlled diabetes, or significantly impaired lung function, your surgeon may recommend optimising these conditions first or steering you toward a non-surgical option. This is not a rejection—it is a safeguard to ensure the path chosen is the safest one for your body.

Sometimes surgery is performed with palliative intent rather than curative intent. If your cancer is advanced, surgery may be used to relieve symptoms such as bowel obstruction, bleeding, or pain, or to reduce the tumour's size so that chemotherapy or radiation works more effectively. This decision is never made lightly. Your surgeon, your family, and the full multidisciplinary team will discuss the goals openly with you so you understand exactly what the surgery can and cannot achieve.

If you have already undergone chemotherapy or radiation as neoadjuvant (pre-operative) treatment, you may still be a candidate for surgery once the tumour has shrunk. Your surgeon will reassess your imaging after neoadjuvant therapy to confirm the tumour is now resectable.

## Pre-Operative Preparation

Preparation for your surgery in Bangalore begins well before the operation date. Once your surgical plan is confirmed, the hospital team will schedule a series of pre-operative assessments—blood tests, an ECG, a chest X-ray, and sometimes an echocardiogram or pulmonary function tests, depending on your age and medical history. These tests are typically completed over 2–3 days at the hospital where your operation will take place.

You will also meet your surgical oncologist for a final pre-operative consultation. This is your opportunity to ask every question on your mind. Your surgeon will explain exactly what will be removed, whether lymph nodes will be sampled, and whether reconstructive surgery is planned. You will be asked to sign an informed consent form only after all your questions have been answered. The anaesthetist will also review your case and discuss the type of anaesthesia planned and how your pain will be managed.

Practical preparations include fasting for 6–8 hours before surgery, stopping certain medications such as blood thinners as advised by your doctor, and arranging for a family member or companion to be present during your hospital stay. ## Procedure Details

Cancer surgery is performed in a dedicated operating theatre under general anaesthesia in most cases, though regional or local anaesthesia may be used for minor procedures. The surgical approach depends on your tumour type, location, and stage. Open surgery uses a single incision over the tumour site for direct access. Laparoscopic surgery uses several small incisions, a camera, and specialised instruments, offering smaller scars and faster recovery. Robotic-assisted surgery with the da Vinci system provides enhanced precision and dexterity, particularly for prostate, gynaecological, colorectal, and head and neck cancers.

The type of resection also varies. Curative resection removes the tumour with clear margins of healthy tissue. Lymph node dissection removes nearby nodes for staging. Debulking surgery reduces tumour volume to enhance other treatments. Reconstructive surgery restores form and function after resection. Your surgical oncologist determines the optimal combination for your case.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the day of surgery, you will be admitted to the hospital and prepared in the pre-operative area. This involves changing into a surgical gown, having an intravenous line placed for fluids and medications, and receiving a pre-medication if prescribed. The anaesthetic team will administer your anaesthesia—general anaesthesia is most common for cancer surgery, though regional or local anaesthesia may be used for certain minor procedures. You will be asleep and will not feel pain during the operation.

Your surgeon then performs the resection according to the planned approach. For open surgery, an incision is made over the tumour site to provide direct access. For laparoscopic or robotic surgery, several small incisions are made through which a camera and specialised instruments are inserted, and your surgeon operates while viewing a high-definition monitor. The tumour is removed along with a margin of surrounding healthy tissue to ensure no cancer cells are left behind. Depending on your cancer type, nearby lymph nodes may also be removed and sent for pathological examination to check for spread.

Throughout the procedure, the anaesthetic team continuously monitors your vital signs. Blood loss is carefully managed, and transfusion is available if needed. For complex or prolonged operations, additional measures such as a urinary catheter, surgical drains, or central venous lines may be placed. Once the resection is complete, your surgeon checks for bleeding, closes the incision in layers, and applies a sterile dressing. If reconstructive surgery is planned, the reconstructive surgeon may take over at this stage.

After the operation, you are transferred to the recovery room where you wake up under close monitoring. Once your vital signs are stable and you are sufficiently awake, you are moved to your hospital room—or to the ICU if the procedure was complex or closer monitoring is required.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

The first 24–48 hours after surgery are the most critical, and you will be closely monitored. Pain management is a priority—no one expects you to simply endure discomfort. You will receive analgesics through an intravenous line, an epidural, or a patient-controlled analgesia pump. Early mobilisation—sitting up, standing, and walking with assistance—is encouraged as soon as it is safe, because movement helps prevent complications such as blood clots, pneumonia, and bowel sluggishness.

Your wound care is managed by the surgical team, with regular dressing changes and monitoring for signs of infection such as redness, swelling, or discharge. If surgical drains have been placed, these are monitored and removed once output decreases to an acceptable level, typically within a few days. You will gradually transition from intravenous fluids to oral intake, starting with sips of water and progressing to a normal diet as your digestive system recovers—particularly after abdominal surgery.

The tissue removed during your operation is sent to the pathology laboratory, and results are typically available within 5–7 days. The pathology report provides critical information: tumour type, grade, margin status, lymph node involvement, and stage. These details determine whether you need additional treatment such as chemotherapy or radiation. Your surgical oncologist will discuss these results with you and the multidisciplinary team, and a plan for any adjuvant therapy will be formulated before you are discharged.

You will likely be discharged from hospital 2–7 days after surgery, depending on the procedure. You will remain in Bangalore for an additional 1–2 weeks for outpatient monitoring, wound review, and to receive your pathology results and any adjuvant treatment plan. ## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

The success of your cancer surgery depends on multiple factors: the cancer type, the stage at diagnosis, the completeness of the surgical resection, and your overall health. For early-stage solid tumours, surgical resection with clear margins can achieve five-year survival rates exceeding 80–90% for cancers such as breast, thyroid, and localised colorectal cancer. For locally advanced cancers, surgery combined with adjuvant chemotherapy or radiation can still offer excellent outcomes. No surgeon can guarantee a cure, but these figures represent realistic expectations based on large-scale clinical data.

Your pathology results play a crucial role in predicting long-term outcomes. Clear margins (no cancer cells at the edge of the removed tissue) and negative lymph nodes are favourable indicators, while positive margins or lymph node involvement may indicate the need for additional treatment. Your oncologist will discuss your pathology results in detail and explain what they mean for your prognosis and follow-up plan—never hesitate to ask for clarification.

Long-term surveillance after cancer surgery typically involves periodic imaging (CT, MRI, or PET-CT) and tumour marker blood tests at intervals determined by your oncologist—usually every 3–6 months for the first two years, then annually. This surveillance is designed to detect any recurrence at the earliest possible stage.

## Alternatives to Consider

While surgery is the most effective treatment for many localised solid tumours, it is not your only option. If you are not fit for surgery due to age, coexisting medical conditions, or tumour location, radiation therapy can offer an equally effective alternative for certain cancers, particularly when delivered with advanced techniques such as SBRT. For some cancers, such as early-stage prostate cancer and certain lymphomas, radiation therapy may be preferred over surgery due to comparable outcomes and different side effect profiles.

Systemic therapies including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy may be used instead of surgery for cancers that have already spread beyond the primary site, or as neoadjuvant treatment to shrink a tumour before surgery becomes feasible. In some cases, a combination of systemic therapy and radiation (chemoradiation) can achieve similar results to surgery while avoiding the need for an operation, particularly in cancers of the larynx, oesophagus, and cervix.

Active surveillance is another alternative for certain slow-growing or low-risk cancers, such as some cases of low-grade prostate cancer. This involves close monitoring with regular PSA tests, biopsies, and imaging, with treatment initiated only if the cancer shows signs of progression. Your multidisciplinary team in Bangalore will discuss all available options and help you choose the approach that best balances cancer control, quality of life, and your personal preferences.
