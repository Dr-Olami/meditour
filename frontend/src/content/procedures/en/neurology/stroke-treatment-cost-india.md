## Overview

A stroke is a medical emergency that changes lives in an instant. Whether it has happened to you or someone you love, the suddenness is frightening — one moment everything is normal, the next, the world has shifted. You do not have to face this alone.

A stroke occurs when the blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted or reduced, depriving brain tissue of oxygen and nutrients. Within minutes, brain cells begin to die. There are two main types: **ischaemic stroke**, which accounts for about 85% of cases and occurs when a blood clot blocks a brain artery, and **haemorrhagic stroke**, which occurs when a weakened blood vessel ruptures and bleeds into or around the brain. A **transient ischaemic attack (TIA)**, sometimes called a mini-stroke, is a temporary blockage that resolves on its own but serves as a critical warning sign.

The most important principle in stroke treatment is that **time is brain**. Every minute of delayed treatment means approximately 1.9 million neurons lost. For ischaemic stroke, intravenous thrombolysis is most effective within 4.5 hours of symptom onset, and mechanical thrombectomy can be performed up to 24 hours in selected patients. For haemorrhagic stroke, rapid blood pressure control and, in some cases, surgical intervention are essential.

Bangalore's leading hospitals have dedicated **stroke units** with multidisciplinary teams — stroke neurologists, interventional neuroradiologists, neurosurgeons, rehabilitation physicians, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, and speech and language therapists — providing emergency treatment and comprehensive rehabilitation. These centres use the same advanced diagnostic and interventional tools as top hospitals in the US and UK, including 24/7 CT and MRI imaging, CT angiography, digital subtraction angiography, and biplane catheterisation laboratories for mechanical thrombectomy.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

You may be reading this because you or a loved one has experienced a stroke, or perhaps a TIA that has served as a warning. Understanding your treatment options can bring clarity and hope. Stroke treatment in Bangalore is appropriate for patients at different stages of the stroke journey:

**For acute stroke**, if you or your loved one is experiencing stroke symptoms — sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body, sudden difficulty speaking or understanding speech, sudden vision loss, sudden severe headache, or sudden loss of balance — emergency treatment is needed immediately. If you are within the therapeutic window, intravenous thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy may be performed to restore blood flow and minimise permanent damage.

**For subacute stroke**, if the acute phase has passed and you are dealing with the aftermath — weakness, speech difficulties, swallowing problems, cognitive changes, or mobility limitations — a comprehensive rehabilitation programme can help you regain function and independence. Early, intensive, multidisciplinary rehabilitation produces the best outcomes.

**For secondary prevention**, if you have had a stroke or TIA and want to reduce the risk of another, Bangalore's stroke specialists can identify the cause — carotid artery stenosis, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, or another factor — and develop a personalised prevention plan including medication, lifestyle modification, and where indicated, procedures such as carotid endarterectomy or stenting.

## Procedure Details

Stroke treatment is not a single procedure — it is a comprehensive programme spanning emergency intervention, acute medical management, and structured rehabilitation. Your treatment plan is developed by a multidisciplinary stroke team based on stroke type, severity, location, and overall health.

**For acute ischaemic stroke**, the treatment pathway depends on the time since symptom onset and the location of the blockage:

*Intravenous thrombolysis* involves administering a clot-busting medication called alteplase (tPA) or tenecteplase through an IV line. This dissolves the clot and restores blood flow. It is most effective within 4.5 hours of symptom onset and can significantly reduce disability if administered promptly. The medication is infused over one hour, with close neurological and vital sign monitoring throughout.

*Mechanical thrombectomy* is an endovascular procedure for large vessel occlusion in the anterior circulation. An interventional neuroradiologist threads a catheter through an artery in the groin up to the blocked brain artery, then uses a stent retriever or aspiration device to physically remove the clot. This can be performed up to 24 hours after symptom onset in selected patients with favourable imaging profiles. The procedure is performed under sedation or general anaesthesia and typically takes 30 to 90 minutes.

**For acute haemorrhagic stroke**, treatment focuses on controlling blood pressure, reducing intracranial pressure, and addressing the source of bleeding. For ruptured aneurysms, **endovascular coil embolisation** or **surgical clipping** may be performed. For large haematomas causing pressure on the brain, **surgical decompression** or **haematoma evacuation** may be necessary.

**For stroke rehabilitation**, the programme is delivered by a multidisciplinary team and typically includes:

*Physiotherapy* focuses on restoring mobility, strength, balance, and coordination. Sessions are tailored to your specific deficits — hemiparesis (weakness on one side), gait disturbance, or balance problems. Techniques include progressive exercise, gait training, constraint-induced movement therapy, and functional electrical stimulation.

*Occupational therapy* focuses on restoring the ability to perform activities of daily living — dressing, eating, bathing, and using the toilet. Therapists work on fine motor skills, cognitive rehabilitation, and the use of adaptive equipment where needed.

*Speech and language therapy* addresses communication difficulties (aphasia) and swallowing problems (dysphagia), common after stroke. Therapy includes exercises to improve articulation, comprehension, reading, and writing, as well as swallowing assessments and dietary modifications.

## What Happens During the Procedure

If you or your loved one is undergoing **intravenous thrombolysis**, the process begins in the emergency department or stroke unit. After a rapid CT brain scan confirms the stroke is ischaemic (not haemorrhagic), the clot-busting medication is prepared based on body weight and infused through an IV line over one hour. Throughout the infusion, nursing staff monitor neurological status, blood pressure, and any signs of bleeding. After the infusion, you remain in the stroke unit for 24 hours of close observation, with repeat CT imaging to check for complications.

If you are undergoing **mechanical thrombectomy**, you will be taken to the catheterisation laboratory. The interventional neuroradiologist will insert a catheter into the femoral artery in your groin and guide it under X-ray guidance to the blocked brain artery. Using a stent retriever — a tiny wire mesh tube that traps the clot — or an aspiration catheter, the clot is removed and blood flow is restored. Your neurological status and vital signs are continuously monitored. After the procedure, you will be transferred to the neurocritical care unit or stroke unit for observation.

If you are undergoing **stroke rehabilitation**, each day typically involves multiple therapy sessions. A typical day might include a morning physiotherapy session working on mobility and strength, an afternoon occupational therapy session focusing on daily living skills, and a speech therapy session if needed. The rehabilitation team meets weekly to review your progress and adjust your programme. Family members are encouraged to participate so they can support your recovery after discharge.

**Will it hurt?** Acute interventions like thrombolysis and thrombectomy are not painful in themselves, though you may feel pressure at the catheter insertion site for thrombectomy. Rehabilitation can be physically demanding with muscle soreness as you rebuild strength, but therapists carefully pace your programme to avoid overexertion.

## Post-operative Care & Recovery

After acute stroke intervention, you will spend the first 24 to 72 hours in the **stroke unit or neurocritical care unit** for close monitoring. The team will check your neurological status frequently — assessing level of consciousness, pupil responses, limb strength, and speech. Your blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen levels are continuously monitored. Fatigue, headache, or mild confusion may occur, managed with medication and supportive care.

Most patients transition from the stroke unit to a general neurology ward after 3 to 5 days, where rehabilitation begins. The intensity of rehabilitation increases gradually as you tolerate it. Your stroke prevention medications are optimised — antiplatelets or anticoagulants, antihypertensives, statins, and antidiabetic medications — to reduce the risk of recurrent stroke.

Most patients are discharged from hospital 7 to 14 days after an acute stroke, depending on severity. Before discharge, you will have a follow-up CT or MRI brain to assess stroke evolution, a neurological assessment to document deficits and recovery, and a detailed rehabilitation plan. You will be given comprehensive instructions on medication, home exercises, dietary modifications, and warning signs requiring immediate medical attention.

## Long-term Outcomes & Success Rates

You are probably wondering how much recovery is possible after a stroke. The answers depend on many factors — the type, location, and severity of the stroke, your age and overall health, and how quickly treatment was received — but they are broadly encouraging, especially with early and intensive rehabilitation.

For patients who receive **intravenous thrombolysis** within 3 hours of symptom onset, approximately 30-50% achieve functional independence at 3 months, compared to about 20-30% of those who do not. For **mechanical thrombectomy** in large vessel occlusion, successful reperfusion is achieved in 70-90% of cases, and approximately 45-60% of patients achieve functional independence at 90 days — a significant improvement over medical therapy alone.

For **stroke rehabilitation**, the greatest recovery occurs in the first 3 to 6 months, though improvement can continue for up to 2 years. Studies show that patients who receive intensive, multidisciplinary rehabilitation in a dedicated stroke unit have significantly better outcomes than those who receive standard care — including greater functional independence, reduced disability, and lower mortality. On average, about 50-70% of stroke survivors regain the ability to walk independently, and 70-80% can perform basic activities of daily living with or without assistance.

Long-term success depends on several factors: continuing stroke prevention medications exactly as prescribed, attending regular follow-up appointments, maintaining lifestyle modifications (smoking cessation, healthy diet, regular exercise, blood pressure and blood sugar control), and continuing rehabilitation exercises at home. Your neurologist will guide you on long-term management and medication adjustments.

## Alternatives to Consider

You might be wondering about options beyond hospital-based rehabilitation. That depends on your specific situation and recovery stage.

For secondary stroke prevention, **lifestyle modifications** are fundamental regardless of other treatments. These include smoking cessation, maintaining a healthy weight, regular aerobic exercise, a Mediterranean-style diet low in sodium and saturated fats, limiting alcohol, and strict control of blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol. These measures can reduce stroke risk by up to 80%.

For patients with **carotid artery stenosis** as the cause, **carotid endarterectomy** (surgical removal of the plaque) or **carotid artery stenting** may be recommended to prevent recurrent stroke. These procedures are available at Bangalore's leading hospitals.

For patients with **atrial fibrillation** as the cause, anticoagulation therapy is essential, and in selected cases, **left atrial appendage closure** (a device-based procedure to prevent clot formation in the heart) may be an alternative to long-term anticoagulation.

For rehabilitation, **home-based therapy programmes** guided by your local rehabilitation team, **community-based stroke support groups**, and **assistive technology** such as functional electrical stimulation devices or robotic-assisted therapy can complement your recovery.
