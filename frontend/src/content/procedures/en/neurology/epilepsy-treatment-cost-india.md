## Overview

Being told you have epilepsy — or that your seizures are not controlled by medication — is frightening and confusing. Questions about what this means for your life, work, and family are natural, and you do not have to navigate this alone.

Epilepsy is a neurological disorder in which the brain's electrical activity becomes abnormal, causing recurrent seizures. Seizures can range from brief lapses in awareness to severe convulsions with loss of consciousness. For approximately 70% of people with epilepsy, anti-seizure medications provide good seizure control. However, about 30% have drug-resistant epilepsy, where medications fail to adequately control seizures. For these patients, advanced treatments including epilepsy surgery, vagus nerve stimulation, and specialised dietary therapies can offer significant improvement or even complete seizure freedom.

Bangalore's leading hospitals have dedicated epilepsy centres with multidisciplinary teams — neurologists, epileptologists, neurosurgeons, neuropsychologists, and specialised EEG technicians — who work together to provide precise diagnosis and personalised treatment. These centres use the same advanced diagnostic tools and surgical techniques as top hospitals in the US and UK, including long-term video-EEG monitoring, high-resolution 3-Tesla MRI, and intraoperative MRI-guided surgery.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

You may be reading this because you or a loved one has been diagnosed with epilepsy and seizures are not adequately controlled. Understanding your treatment options can bring real hope.

Epilepsy treatment in Bangalore is appropriate for patients with **confirmed epilepsy** — meaning you have had two or more unprovoked seizures. If newly diagnosed, a comprehensive evaluation will determine your seizure type and the most appropriate first-line medication. If you have already tried medications and seizures persist, you may have drug-resistant epilepsy, which requires advanced evaluation and potentially surgical treatment.

You may be a candidate for **epilepsy surgery** if you have failed at least two appropriately chosen and tolerated anti-seizure medications. The surgical evaluation aims to identify where your seizures originate in the brain. If a single seizure focus is found in a region that can be safely removed or disconnected, surgery can offer a high probability of seizure freedom — particularly for temporal lobe epilepsy, where success rates exceed 60-80%.

If your seizures arise from multiple areas of the brain or the focus cannot be safely localised, **neurostimulation therapies** such as vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) or deep brain stimulation (DBS) may be appropriate. These do not cure epilepsy but can significantly reduce seizure frequency and severity.

## Pre-operative Preparation

Before you travel to Bangalore, gather all your medical records — previous EEG reports, MRI scans (on CD or film), seizure diaries documenting frequency and characteristics, and a complete list of every medication you have tried and are currently taking. Share these with your neurologist in advance so the epilepsy team can review your case and plan your evaluation before you arrive.

Your doctor will likely ask you to continue your current anti-seizure medications until you arrive, as abrupt discontinuation can trigger severe seizures. Be completely honest about everything you take, including herbal supplements, because some can interact with anti-seizure drugs or lower seizure threshold.

Once you arrive in Bangalore, you will undergo a comprehensive evaluation. This typically includes prolonged **video-EEG monitoring** — staying in a dedicated epilepsy monitoring unit for several days with continuous EEG recording and video capture to record your typical seizures. You may have your medications reduced during this monitoring to increase the likelihood of seizure capture. You will also have a **high-resolution MRI brain** (often 3-Tesla with epilepsy protocol), **neuropsychological testing** to map your cognitive strengths and weaknesses, and possibly **PET or SPECT imaging** to help localise the seizure focus.

For surgical candidates, additional tests may include **functional MRI** to map critical brain functions near the planned surgical site, **Wada testing** to assess language and memory lateralisation, and an **anaesthesia consultation** to assess fitness for surgery.

## Procedure Details

Epilepsy treatment is not a single procedure — it is a comprehensive programme tailored to your specific seizure type, frequency, and underlying cause. Your treatment plan is developed by a multidisciplinary epilepsy team after thorough diagnostic evaluation.

**For newly diagnosed or medication-responsive epilepsy**, treatment involves selecting the most appropriate anti-seizure medication based on your seizure type. Your neurologist will start at a low dose and gradually increase it, monitoring for seizure control and side effects. Sometimes a combination is needed. Blood tests monitor drug levels and side effects.

**For drug-resistant epilepsy**, the treatment pathway involves advanced evaluation and one of several approaches:

_Epilepsy surgery_ is the most definitive treatment for drug-resistant focal epilepsy. The most common procedure is a **temporal lobectomy** — removing the portion of the temporal lobe where seizures originate. Other procedures include **lesionectomy** (removing a structural abnormality such as a tumour or scar), **corpus callosotomy** (severing the connection between brain hemispheres to prevent seizure spread in generalised epilepsy), and **hemispherectomy** (for severe cases affecting one entire hemisphere). Surgery is performed under general anaesthesia and typically takes 3-5 hours.

_Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS)_ involves implanting a small device under the skin in your chest, connected to a wire that delivers regular electrical pulses to your vagus nerve in the neck. This does not cure epilepsy but can reduce seizure frequency by 30-50% in many patients. The implantation is a relatively minor surgical procedure taking about 1-2 hours.

_Ketogenic diet therapy_ is primarily used for children with drug-resistant epilepsy, particularly certain genetic and metabolic epilepsy syndromes. This high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet alters brain metabolism in ways that can reduce seizures. It requires careful supervision by a dietitian and neurologist.

## What Happens During the Procedure

If you are undergoing **video-EEG monitoring**, you will be admitted to a dedicated epilepsy monitoring unit. Electrodes are glued to your scalp in a specific pattern, and you will be connected to continuous EEG recording equipment. A video camera records your activity 24 hours a day. You will stay in this unit for several days — typically 3 to 7 — while the team waits to capture your typical seizures. Your medications may be reduced or stopped during this period to increase the chance of seizure capture. You will be closely monitored by nursing staff and the epilepsy team throughout.

If you are undergoing **epilepsy surgery**, on the day of your operation you will be admitted to the pre-operative area. The nursing team will start your IV lines, give you antibiotics, and attach monitoring equipment. The anaesthesiologist will place you under general anaesthesia. Depending on the planned procedure, your neurosurgeon may use **intraoperative MRI** or **neuronavigation** to precisely locate the seizure focus and map critical brain areas. For some cases, **awake craniotomy** is performed — you are briefly awakened during surgery to test language and motor functions while the surgeon stimulates specific brain areas, ensuring critical functions are preserved.

For **vagus nerve stimulation**, the procedure is performed under general anaesthesia. A small incision is made in your left upper chest to create a pocket for the pulse generator, and a second incision in your neck to access the vagus nerve. The lead wire is wrapped around the nerve and connected to the generator, then programmed to deliver electrical pulses at the appropriate settings.

## Post-operative Care & Recovery

After video-EEG monitoring, your medications will be restarted and adjusted based on the findings. You will typically be discharged within a day or two of completing the monitoring. The epilepsy team will discuss the results with you and outline your treatment plan.

After epilepsy surgery, you will spend the first night in the neurosurgical ICU for close monitoring of your neurological status — checking your level of consciousness, pupil responses, limb strength, and speech. You may experience some headache, nausea, and fatigue, which are managed with medication. Most patients transfer to the neurosurgical ward on the second day, where you will gradually increase your activity. Your anti-seizure medications will be continued — they are not stopped immediately after surgery, as the brain needs time to heal.

**Will it hurt?** There will be some discomfort at the surgical site, but this is very manageable with medication and improves significantly within the first few days. You will receive both IV and oral pain medications.

Most patients are discharged from hospital 5 to 7 days after epilepsy surgery. Before discharge, you will have a post-operative MRI to confirm the extent of resection and a neurological assessment to check for any new deficits. You will be given detailed instructions on wound care, medication schedule, and activity restrictions.

After VNS implantation, recovery is quicker — most patients go home within 1 to 2 days. The device is typically activated 1 to 2 weeks after implantation, and the settings are gradually adjusted over subsequent visits to optimise seizure control.

## Long-term Outcomes & Success Rates

You are probably wondering whether epilepsy treatment will actually work. The answers depend on your specific situation, but they are broadly reassuring.

For medication-responsive epilepsy, about 60-70% of patients achieve good seizure control with appropriate anti-seizure medications. Finding the right medication or combination may take several adjustments, but most people live full, active lives with well-controlled epilepsy.

For epilepsy surgery, the outcomes are particularly encouraging for well-selected patients. **Temporal lobectomy** — the most common epilepsy surgery — achieves seizure freedom in 60-80% of patients at one year, with many remaining seizure-free long-term. Studies show early surgery (after failure of two medications) provides better outcomes than continued medication trials. Patients who achieve seizure freedom often experience significant improvements in quality of life, employment, and independence.

For vagus nerve stimulation, approximately 30-50% of patients experience a meaningful reduction in seizure frequency, though complete seizure freedom is less common. The benefits may increase over the first 6 to 12 months as the device settings are optimised.

Long-term success depends on continuing your anti-seizure medications as prescribed — even after successful surgery, medications are typically continued for at least 1 to 2 years and sometimes longer. Your neurologist will guide you on any future medication adjustments.

Before you leave Bangalore, you will receive a comprehensive discharge summary explaining your diagnosis, the treatment performed, medications prescribed, and recommended follow-up. Share this with your neurologist back home. A follow-up EEG and neurology review are typically scheduled at 3 to 6 months after treatment, with regular check-ups at least once a year.

Taking your anti-seizure medications exactly as prescribed is critical. Never stop or change your medication without talking to your neurologist first, as abrupt discontinuation can trigger severe seizures or status epilepticus. If you experience new or worsening seizures, significant side effects, or signs of infection, seek medical attention immediately.

## Alternatives to Consider

You might be wondering if there are options beyond medications and surgery. That depends on your specific epilepsy type.

For some patients, **ketogenic or modified Atkins diet** may be effective, particularly for children with specific epilepsy syndromes. These diets require careful medical and dietetic supervision but can reduce seizures in drug-resistant cases.

**Lifestyle modifications** — adequate sleep, avoiding known seizure triggers such as alcohol and sleep deprivation, and managing stress — complement medical treatment and reduce seizure frequency. These are important for all epilepsy patients.

For patients who are not surgical candidates, **neurostimulation therapies** beyond VNS — including responsive neurostimulation (RNS) and deep brain stimulation (DBS) — may be available at select centres. These newer technologies detect and respond to abnormal brain activity in real time. A shorter initial assessment visit with home programme and tele-consultation follow-up can be arranged for those unable to travel for an extended period.
