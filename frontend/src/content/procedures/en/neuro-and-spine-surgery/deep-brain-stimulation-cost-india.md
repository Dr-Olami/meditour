## Overview

If you or a loved one is living with Parkinson's disease, essential tremor or dystonia, you know how deeply these conditions can affect daily life. The tremor that makes it impossible to hold a cup of tea. The rigidity that turns a simple walk into a struggle. The involuntary movements that draw stares from strangers. If medications are no longer controlling your symptoms — or if the side effects have become nearly as disabling as the disease itself — you may be wondering whether there is another option. Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) is not a cure, but for the right patient, it can be genuinely life-changing.

DBS is an advanced neuromodulation therapy that uses surgically implanted electrodes to deliver precisely controlled electrical pulses to specific regions of your brain. It is the gold-standard surgical treatment for advanced Parkinson's disease, essential tremor and dystonia in patients whose symptoms are no longer adequately controlled by medication. DBS can dramatically reduce tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia and dystonic movements, significantly improving your quality of life.

The procedure involves implanting thin electrodes into targeted brain structures — most commonly the subthalamic nucleus (STN) or globus pallidus internus (GPi) for Parkinson's disease, the ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) of the thalamus for essential tremor, and the GPi for dystonia. These electrodes are connected by extension wires to an implantable pulse generator (IPG) placed under your skin in the chest or abdomen, similar to a pacemaker. The device is externally programmable, allowing your neurologist to adjust stimulation settings non-invasively over time.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

DBS is primarily indicated if you have Parkinson's disease, essential tremor or dystonia with a confirmed diagnosis from a neurologist, and your symptoms are no longer adequately controlled by optimised medication therapy. For Parkinson's disease, a key eligibility criterion is a significant improvement in your symptoms during a levodopa challenge test, which demonstrates that your motor symptoms are levodopa-responsive and therefore likely to benefit from stimulation.

If you have essential tremor and experience disabling tremor despite first-line medications such as propranolol or primidone, you are an excellent candidate for thalamic DBS. The procedure can dramatically reduce tremor amplitude, restoring your ability to perform daily activities such as writing, eating and drinking. If you have dystonia — including cervical dystonia, generalised dystonia or tardive dystonia — GPi-DBS can significantly reduce abnormal postures and involuntary movements.

Importantly, you must not have severe cognitive impairment or untreated psychiatric conditions, as these can be worsened by DBS and may compromise your ability to participate in programming sessions. A thorough neuropsychological evaluation is part of the pre-operative assessment. You must also have realistic expectations about the degree of symptom improvement and a commitment to long-term follow-up.

## Procedure Details

DBS surgery is typically performed in two stages. In the first stage, you are fitted with a stereotactic head frame and undergo an MRI to map the target brain region with sub-millimetre accuracy. Under local anaesthesia, the neurosurgeon inserts the electrodes through small burr holes in your skull. Because you are awake, the surgical team can perform microelectrode recording and test stimulation to confirm optimal electrode placement and assess for any side effects. In some centres, this stage is performed under general anaesthesia using intraoperative MRI guidance.

In the second stage, performed either immediately or a few days later, the extension wires are tunnelled under your skin from the scalp to the chest, where the implantable pulse generator is placed in a subcutaneous pocket. This stage is performed under general anaesthesia. The device is typically switched on 2–4 weeks after surgery to allow your brain to recover from the immediate effects of implantation.

DBS programming is a critical part of your treatment and requires multiple sessions over several weeks to optimise stimulation parameters. Your neurologist adjusts amplitude, frequency, pulse width and electrode configuration to maximise symptom control while minimising side effects. The choice of device — Medtronic, Boston Scientific or Abbott — depends on your specific condition, your neurologist's preference, and the available programming infrastructure in your home country.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the morning of the first stage, the stereotactic head frame is applied under local anaesthesia. You undergo an MRI with the frame in place to precisely localise the target brain structure. In the operating theatre, the neurosurgeon makes small burr holes in your skull and advances the electrode toward the target using stereotactic coordinates. For awake DBS, you remain conscious and cooperative throughout, allowing the team to perform microelectrode recording and test stimulation to confirm symptom improvement and check for side effects.

**Will it hurt?** You will be given local anaesthesia for the burr holes, so you should not feel pain during electrode placement — though you may feel some pressure. You will be able to communicate with the surgical team throughout, which is essential for testing symptom response.

Once optimal electrode position is confirmed, the electrode is secured and the incision is closed. The second stage — connecting the electrodes to the pulse generator — is performed under general anaesthesia either immediately or a few days later. Extension wires are tunnelled under your skin from the scalp behind the ear to the chest, where the implantable pulse generator is placed in a subcutaneous pocket below your collarbone. This stage typically takes 1–2 hours.

After surgery, you will be monitored in the neurosurgical ward for 24–48 hours. A CT scan is performed to confirm electrode position and rule out any bleeding. The device is left switched off for 2–4 weeks to allow your brain tissue to settle around the electrodes.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

The initial recovery period involves wound care and pain management at the surgical sites — your scalp, behind the ear and your chest. Most patients stay in hospital for 5–10 days, during which the neurosurgical team monitors for any complications such as bleeding, infection or hardware issues. You are encouraged to mobilise gently and resume your normal medication regimen as directed by the neurologist.

The first DBS programming session typically takes place 2–4 weeks after surgery, once your brain has settled. This session is conducted by the movement disorder neurologist and involves initial activation of the device, assessment of stimulation-induced benefits and side effects, and selection of preliminary settings. Multiple programming sessions over the following weeks are needed to fine-tune the parameters, and your medications are gradually adjusted in parallel.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

DBS has well-established long-term efficacy for its primary indications. In Parkinson's disease, STN-DBS typically improves motor function by 50–70% as measured by the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS), with significant reductions in medication requirements and medication-induced dyskinesias. Benefits are sustained over 5–10 years of follow-up, although the disease continues to progress and non-motor symptoms such as speech, balance and cognition may not improve and can worsen over time.

For essential tremor, thalamic DBS can reduce tremor by 80–90% on the stimulated side, often producing dramatic functional improvements. For dystonia, GPi-DBS can reduce dystonia severity by 50–80%, with improvements that may continue to develop over months to years after implantation. The long-term success of DBS depends heavily on consistent programming, medication management and regular follow-up.

## Alternatives to Consider

Before proceeding with DBS, you should ensure that all reasonable medication options have been explored. For Parkinson's disease, this includes optimising levodopa dosing, adding adjuncts such as MAO-B inhibitors, COMT inhibitors or dopamine agonists, and considering advanced delivery methods such as levodopa-carbidopa intestinal gel (LCIG) or apomorphine infusion.

For essential tremor, medication options include propranolol, primidone, gabapentin and topiramate, as well as botulinum toxin injections. Focused ultrasound thalamotomy (MRgFUS) is a newer non-invasive alternative that uses focused sound waves to create a lesion in the thalamus, providing tremor reduction without an implanted device — though it is irreversible and typically unilateral. For dystonia, treatment alternatives include botulinum toxin injections, oral medications and intrathecal baclofen pumps.
