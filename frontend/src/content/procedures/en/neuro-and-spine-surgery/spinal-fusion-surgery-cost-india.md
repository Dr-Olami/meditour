## Overview

If you have been living with chronic back or leg pain that has not responded to physiotherapy, medications or injections, you may be reaching the point where surgery feels like your only remaining option. The decision to have spinal fusion is significant — you are probably wondering whether it will really relieve your pain, how long recovery will take, and whether you will be able to return to the activities you love. These are all completely natural concerns, and this guide will help you understand what spinal fusion in Bangalore involves so you can make an informed decision.

Spinal fusion is a surgical procedure that permanently joins two or more of your vertebrae to eliminate painful motion and restore stability to your spine. It is commonly performed for degenerative disc disease, spondylolisthesis (a slipped vertebra), spinal stenosis, spinal fractures and certain deformities such as scoliosis. The surgery uses bone grafts — taken from you, a donor, or synthetic material — along with hardware such as screws, rods and cages to hold the vertebrae together while the fusion heals into a single solid unit over several months.

India has become a preferred destination for spinal fusion surgery, offering costs of $5,000–$15,000 compared to $50,000–$150,000 in the United States. Bangalore's spine surgery hospitals combine advanced intraoperative imaging, neuro-monitoring and minimally invasive capabilities with highly experienced spine surgeons, delivering excellent clinical outcomes at a fraction of Western prices. The cost advantage is driven by lower infrastructure and labour costs, not by any compromise in surgical expertise or technology.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

Spinal fusion surgery may be recommended if you have confirmed spinal instability or progressive deformity on imaging and have failed at least 3–6 months of conservative treatment including physiotherapy and medication. Common indications include spondylolisthesis, degenerative disc disease with instability, recurrent disc herniation, spinal stenosis requiring decompression and stabilisation, spinal fractures and certain deformities such as degenerative scoliosis.

If you have persistent back or leg pain that significantly affects your quality of life and has not responded to non-surgical measures, you may be a typical candidate. The pain may be mechanical (worse with movement and loading) or radicular (shooting pain down your leg due to nerve compression). A thorough evaluation including dynamic X-rays, MRI and sometimes CT helps the surgeon determine whether instability is present and fusion is appropriate.

You must be medically fit for general anaesthesia and a major surgical procedure, with adequate cardiovascular, respiratory and renal function, no active infection, and no uncontrolled systemic conditions. Adequate bone quality is important to support fusion and implant fixation; if you have osteoporosis, you may require bone grafting or augmentation.

You should have realistic expectations. Spinal fusion can significantly reduce your pain and improve function, but the fused segment no longer moves, which can increase stress on adjacent levels over time.

## Procedure Details

Spinal fusion surgery is performed under general anaesthesia with you positioned face down. The surgeon makes an incision over the affected spinal segment and carefully retracts the muscles to expose your vertebrae. Using fluoroscopic guidance, pedicle screws are placed into your vertebral bodies and connected by rods to stabilise your spine. The intervertebral disc is removed and replaced with a cage packed with bone graft, which encourages the two vertebrae to fuse into a single solid unit over several months.

Minimally invasive variants such as transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) use smaller incisions, muscle-splitting tubular retractors and percutaneous screw placement. These techniques reduce blood loss, post-operative pain and hospital stay, and are increasingly available at Bangalore's leading spine centres. Other approaches include XLIF (extreme lateral interbody fusion) and ALIF (anterior lumbar interbody fusion), each suited to different clinical scenarios. Intraoperative neuromonitoring is used throughout to protect your spinal cord and nerve roots.

The bone graft material may be autograft (taken from your own hip or local bone), allograft (donor bone) or synthetic bone graft substitutes. Autograft provides the best fusion rates as it contains living bone cells, but harvesting from your hip adds a second surgical site. Your surgeon will discuss the most appropriate graft option based on the number of levels fused and your bone quality.

After surgery, you will spend 1–2 days in the neuro-ICU or HDU before transferring to the ward. Early mobilisation with a spinal brace is encouraged. Full bony fusion typically takes 3–6 months, during which you must avoid heavy lifting, twisting and high-impact activities. The hardware remains in place permanently even after fusion is complete, providing ongoing structural support.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the day of surgery, you will be admitted to the pre-operative area where the anaesthesia team places intravenous lines, applies monitoring equipment and administers general anaesthesia. You will be positioned face down on a specialised spinal operating table that allows fluoroscopic imaging from multiple angles.

The surgeon exposes the affected segment, places pedicle screws under fluoroscopic guidance (and computer-assisted navigation where available), and connects them with rods to provide immediate stability. For an interbody fusion, the diseased disc is removed and a cage packed with bone graft is inserted into the disc space, restoring disc height and decompressing the nerve roots.

Intraoperative neuromonitoring continuously assesses your spinal cord and nerve root function throughout the procedure, alerting the surgeon if any manipulation risks causing a deficit. Once the hardware is in place and the bone graft is positioned, the surgeon verifies alignment with final imaging, achieves haemostasis and closes the incision in layers. The procedure typically lasts 2–4 hours depending on the number of levels fused and complexity.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

After surgery, you will be monitored in the neuro-ICU or HDU for 1–2 days. Nurses and doctors check your neurological function frequently — assessing limb strength, sensation and wound site — to detect any early signs of complications. Pain is managed with intravenous analgesics initially, transitioning to oral medications as you stabilise.

**Will it hurt?** You will have significant surgical site pain in the first few days, but it is well managed with a multimodal analgesia protocol and gradually subsides over the first week. Once stable, you will be transferred to the neurosurgical ward. A spinal brace is typically fitted to support your spine during early mobilisation. Physiotherapy begins within 1–2 days of surgery, starting with gentle mobilisation and progressing to walking and basic functional exercises. A follow-up X-ray is performed before discharge to confirm hardware position and alignment.

During your Bangalore recovery period of 1–2 weeks, physiotherapy sessions continue on an outpatient basis, focusing on core stability, gentle range of motion and safe mobilisation with the brace. Your surgical wound is reviewed and stitches or staples are removed. You will receive a detailed home exercise programme and activity guidelines, including restrictions on bending, lifting, twisting and high-impact activities for 3–6 months until solid fusion is confirmed on imaging.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

Spinal fusion surgery has well-established clinical outcomes for its primary indications. For spondylolisthesis and degenerative instability, fusion success rates of 85–95% are reported, with significant improvements in back pain, leg pain and functional scores. Minimally invasive techniques have been shown to reduce complications, blood loss and hospital stay while achieving comparable fusion rates to open surgery.

The primary long-term concern after spinal fusion is adjacent segment degeneration, in which the vertebrae above and below the fused level wear out prematurely due to increased mechanical stress. This occurs in approximately 10–20% of patients over 10 years and may require extension of the fusion. The risk is higher with multilevel fusions and in patients with poor bone quality or ongoing risk factors such as smoking.

Full bony fusion typically takes 3–6 months, confirmed by X-ray or CT imaging. Once fusion is complete, most patients can return to full activity including sport, though contact sports and heavy lifting may be discouraged long-term.

## Alternatives to Consider

Before proceeding with spinal fusion, you should ensure all reasonable conservative options have been explored, including physiotherapy, activity modification, weight management, NSAIDs, epidural steroid injections and facet joint injections. Many patients achieve meaningful symptom relief with these measures and may not require surgery.

Disc replacement surgery is a motion-preserving alternative for carefully selected patients with single or two-level disc disease and no significant instability or facet arthritis. By maintaining motion at the treated level, disc replacement may reduce the risk of adjacent segment degeneration compared to fusion. However, it is not suitable if you have spondylolisthesis, stenosis requiring decompression, or multilevel disease. For patients not fit for surgery or who prefer to avoid it, pain management programmes combining medications, injections and rehabilitation can provide sustained symptom control.
