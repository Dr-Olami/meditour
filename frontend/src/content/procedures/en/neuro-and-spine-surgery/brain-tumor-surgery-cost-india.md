## Overview

Being told you have a brain tumor is one of the most frightening things you can hear. You may be feeling scared, confused, and desperate for clear answers. Take a breath. You are not alone in this, and there are options available that combine world-class surgical care with costs far more manageable than you might expect.

Brain tumor surgery is a highly specialised procedure that removes all or part of a tumour from your brain while protecting your critical functions — speech, movement, vision, memory. The approach depends on your tumour's size, location, and type, as well as your overall health. It may involve an open craniotomy, microsurgical resection, or a minimally invasive endoscopic technique. Bangalore is home to several JCI-accredited hospitals equipped with intraoperative MRI, neuronavigation systems, and advanced neuro-ICUs.

The cost of brain tumour surgery in India ranges from $6,000 to $20,000, compared to $50,000-$150,000 in the United States — a saving of 70-80% without lower quality. Indian neurosurgeons are globally trained, and top hospitals report outcomes comparable to leading Western institutions. You also benefit from short waiting times, English-speaking medical teams, and dedicated international patient services.

Your tumour may be primary or metastatic, benign or malignant. For benign tumours, complete removal can be curative. For malignant tumours, the goal is to remove as much as safely possible, followed by radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Your surgical plan will always be tailored to your specific situation.

## Who Is This Procedure For?

This surgery is for you if you have a confirmed diagnosis of a primary or metastatic brain tumour on MRI or CT imaging, and your tumour is surgically accessible. If your tumour is causing pressure on surrounding brain tissue — headaches, seizures, weakness, or visual disturbances — you will often benefit most from prompt surgical intervention.

You need to be medically stable enough to undergo general anaesthesia and craniotomy. Conditions like diabetes or high blood pressure should be under control.

If you have a benign tumour such as a meningioma, acoustic neuroma, or pituitary adenoma, you are an excellent candidate because complete removal can be curative. If you have a malignant tumour such as a glioblastoma or a metastatic lesion, surgery is usually part of a broader treatment plan combining removal with radiotherapy and chemotherapy.

It is important to have realistic expectations. Some tumours cannot be completely removed without risking permanent damage, and in those cases, your surgeon may recommend removing only part of the tumour or performing a biopsy. ## Pre-Operative Preparation

Before you travel to India, gather a complete set of your medical records — recent MRI scans (with and without contrast), pathology reports if a biopsy has been performed, and a full list of current medications. Khan Meditour can arrange a remote consultation with your neurosurgeon to review these and confirm the surgical plan before you leave home.

You will typically be advised to stop blood-thinning medications such as aspirin, clopidogrel, or warfarin 5-7 days before surgery, but only under your treating physician's guidance. If you have diabetes or hypertension, make sure these are well controlled.

When you arrive, you will undergo a comprehensive pre-operative evaluation including blood tests, an ECG, a chest X-ray, and a repeat MRI if your existing imaging is more than a few weeks old. Your anaesthetist will review your fitness for general anaesthesia, and your neurosurgeon will conduct a detailed neurological examination. You will be asked to fast for 8 hours before surgery.

## Procedure Details

Understanding what will happen during your surgery can help ease the fear. Before any incision, your surgical team takes detailed images using contrast MRI and functional MRI to map the tumour's relationship to critical brain regions. Your neurosurgeon may use neuronavigation, a GPS-like system for your brain, to plan the safest route.

In some cases, your surgeon may recommend an awake craniotomy — one of the safest approaches for tumours near critical areas. You will be sedated during the opening and closing but awake during tumour removal, allowing the surgeon to test your speech or movement in real time.

During a standard craniotomy, a section of your skull is temporarily removed. Using an operating microscope and microsurgical instruments, your surgeon carefully separates and removes the tumour, aiming for maximum safe removal. Intraoperative neuromonitoring continuously checks your nerve and pathway function. For pituitary tumours, an endoscopic approach through your nose means no external incision.

After the tumour is removed, the bone flap is replaced and secured, and you are transferred to the neuro-ICU. Depending on your pathology results, your team may recommend additional treatment such as radiotherapy or chemotherapy.

Bangalore hospitals offer advanced technologies including intraoperative MRI to verify tumour removal during the operation, 5-ALA fluorescent dye that makes high-grade glioma cells glow under blue light, and cortical mapping and awake craniotomy techniques that reduce the risk of permanent deficits.

## What Happens During the Procedure

On the day of your surgery, you will be admitted to the pre-operative area where the anaesthesia team places your intravenous lines and applies monitoring equipment. Your neurosurgeon will mark the surgical site and confirm the planned incision using neuronavigation data. If you are having an awake craniotomy, the anaesthesia team will use a sedation technique that keeps you comfortable during the opening and closing but allows you to be awake during tumour removal.

You will be positioned on the operating table with your head secured in a fixation device. After your scalp is prepared and draped, your surgeon makes an incision and temporarily removes a section of your skull (the bone flap). The protective membrane called the dura is opened, and your surgeon uses an operating microscope and microsurgical instruments to carefully dissect and remove the tumour.

Throughout the removal, intraoperative neuromonitoring continuously checks your nerve and pathway function. If you are having an awake craniotomy, you will be asked to perform tasks such as counting, naming objects, or moving your limbs while specific brain areas are stimulated, allowing your surgeon to map and preserve critical functions in real time.

Once the tumour has been removed to the maximum safe extent, your surgeon ensures bleeding is controlled, closes the dura, replaces the bone flap, and closes your scalp in layers. You are then transferred to the neuro-ICU. The entire procedure typically lasts 3-6 hours.

## Post-Operative Care & Recovery

After your surgery, you will be admitted to the neuro-ICU for 24-48 hours of close monitoring. This is standard and does not mean something is wrong. Nurses and doctors will check your neurological function frequently — pupil reactivity, limb strength, speech, and consciousness — to detect any early signs of complications. Your pain will be managed with intravenous medication, and you will be given anticonvulsant medication to prevent post-operative seizures.

**Will it hurt?** You will have some pain around the incision site, but it is typically well controlled with medication. Most patients describe it as more of a headache and soreness than severe pain.

Once you are stable, you will be transferred to the neurosurgical ward for the remainder of your hospital stay — typically 5-10 days. A CT or MRI scan will usually be performed within 24-48 hours to confirm the extent of tumour removal. Early mobilisation is encouraged with assistance from nursing staff and physiotherapists.

Depending on your tumour type and pathology results, your neurosurgeon will discuss whether you need additional treatment such as radiotherapy or chemotherapy. If you need rehabilitation — physiotherapy for weakness or speech therapy — sessions will begin during your hospital stay and continue on an outpatient basis after discharge.

## Long-Term Outcomes & Success Rates

It is natural to want honest answers about what to expect long term. Your outcome depends heavily on your tumour type, location, and how much could be safely removed.

For benign tumours such as meningiomas and pituitary adenomas, complete surgical removal offers excellent long-term outcomes. Cure rates exceed 90% for completely resected meningiomas, and most patients return to full functional independence within a few months.

For malignant tumours, surgery is one part of a broader treatment strategy. In glioblastoma, maximal safe removal followed by radiotherapy and temozolomide chemotherapy is the standard of care. Greater extent of removal is associated with improved survival. Median survival with this approach is approximately 15-20 months, with some patients living significantly longer, particularly those with favourable molecular markers such as MGMT promoter methylation.

For metastatic brain tumours, surgical removal followed by stereotactic radiosurgery can provide good local control and preserve neurological function. Ongoing surveillance imaging is essential to detect any recurrence.

You will typically be advised to have a follow-up MRI scan 2-3 months after surgery, and then at regular intervals. Anticonvulsant medication may be continued for several months or longer. Monitor yourself for any new neurological symptoms — headaches, seizures, weakness, or visual changes — and seek medical attention promptly if these occur.

## Alternatives to Consider

Not every brain tumour requires surgery, and you deserve to understand all your options before making a decision.

For small tumours that are not causing symptoms — particularly if you are older or have other significant medical conditions — a watch-and-wait approach with regular MRI monitoring may be appropriate.

Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), such as Gamma Knife or CyberKnife, is a non-invasive alternative for small tumours or tumours in locations difficult to reach surgically. SRS delivers highly focused radiation while sparing surrounding brain tissue, and is effective for tumours up to approximately 3 cm. It is particularly useful for metastatic tumours, acoustic neuromas, and recurrent meningiomas. If you are not fit enough for surgery, a biopsy alone may be performed to establish a diagnosis, followed by radiotherapy or chemotherapy.
